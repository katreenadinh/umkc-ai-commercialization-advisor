an impact. For universities and research 
institutions, transforming a groundbreaking idea into

Understanding Market Analysis 
“Market analysis” is the process of understanding 
the environment in which an innovation will exist. How you define the market provides a compass to 
guide your innovation through the competitive landscape and towards real-world use. It combines 
quantitative data (market size, revenue projections) with qualitative insights (consumer values, 
buying motives) to forecast the potential impact of your product or service. 
 
A thorough market analysis will identify the following elements: 
1. Target Industry: Dive deep into industry trends, market size, and the regulatory landscape 
to understand where your innovation fits. 
2. Competitive Landscape: Identify key players, analyze their strengths and weaknesses, and 
uncover opportunities to differentiate your product. 
3. Identification of Market Gaps: Look for unmet needs and underserved segments that your 
innovation can address. 
4. Definition of Target Market: Segment your potential customers based on demographics, 
psychographics, and behavioral patterns. 
5. Barriers to Entry: Assess challenges such as startup costs, regulatory requirements, and 
competitive pressures. 
6. Sales Forecast: Project future sales to inform your business strategy and attract potential 
investors.

Conducting Market Research 
Market research helps test whether your work connects to a real need outside the university. It is 
your window into the minds of potential customers. By gathering and analyzing data, you can 
validate your product’s viability and refine your go-to-market strategy. 
 
Market research fits into two categories: 
- Primary Research: Data collected directly through surveys, focus groups, and interviews to 
gain first-hand insights. 
- Secondary Research: Data collected from industry reports, academic studies, and 
government statistics to inform your analysis. 
 
Primary Research 
Primary research is used to learn about the specific pain points and needs that relate to values your 
invention can address. Surveys give statistical backing, focus groups provide collective insights, 
and interviews offer deep, individualized perspectives. These tools provide quantifiable and 
qualitative measures for how your invention will solve problems, giving you data to define the 
commercial potential of your invention. 
 
Developing a Survey 
Surveys are a cost-effective and easy-to-implement option to create quantifiable data from a broad 
audience of potential users.  
 
To develop an effective survey, consider these tips: 
1. Define Objectives Clearly – Identify what specific information you need (e.g., customer 
pain points, willingness to pay, competitive landscape). 
2. Keep It Concise – Shorter surveys (5–10 minutes) have higher completion rates. 
3. Use a Mix of Question Types: 
a. Closed-ended: Multiple choice, Likert scales (1-5 or 1-7), ranking. (Author’s Note: I 
recommend an even number of choices on Likert scales. Instead of providing a 
middle option that is the equivalent of “no choice,” an even number of responses (ie, 
1-4 or 1-6) forces respondents to choose a positive or negative response, better 
relating to your evaluation.) 
b. Open-ended: Allow respondents to provide insights in their own words. 
4. Avoid Leading Questions – Ensure neutrality in your questions (e.g., "What challenges do 
you face?" instead of "Do you struggle with X problem?"). Poorly worded or leading 
questions can lead to skewed results for your survey.  
5. Segment Your Audience – Identify ideal respondents based on industry, job role, and 
experience level. Understand the roles these individuals will play in purchasing, using, or 
benefitting from your invention.

6. Pilot Test the Survey – Run a small test with a sample group before a full launch to identify 
issues. 
7. Choose the Right Distribution Method – Email lists, industry forums, LinkedIn groups, or 
professional associations. UMKC has several survey tools at our disposal; check with your 
department, IT lead, or contact the University libraries to learn more. 
8. Offer an Incentive (If Possible) – Small incentives like gift cards or entry into a prize draw 
improve response rates. It is NOT necessary for them to be expensive; people will complete 
surveys for a chance to win a $5 gift card. Make it something relevant and valuable for the 
audience you’re targeting, and you’ll increase the likelihood of hitting response targets.  
 
Leading a Focus Group 
Focus groups provide qualitative insights and deeper understanding of potential customer 
perspectives. The goal is to get potential customers to share opinions that could influence their 
decision to adopt your invention.  
 
To ensure a successful session: 
1. Select the Right Participants – Aim for 6-10 individuals who represent your target market 
(diversity in roles and perspectives is beneficial). Seek individuals that could be decision 
makers, end users, or beneficiaries of your invention and learn what is important to them. 
2. Define Clear Goals – Outline specific discussion topics (e.g., usability of your invention, 
market needs, pricing expectations). 
3. Prepare a Discussion Guide – Structure the conversation with open-ended questions to 
encourage dialogue. 
4. Use a Skilled Moderator – Someone experienced in leading discussions should guide the 
group to prevent bias and dominance by a single voice. 
5. Encourage Honest Feedback – Create a comfortable environment where participants feel 
safe sharing honest opinions. 
6. Record (With Permission) – Video or audio recordings help ensure accurate analysis of 
responses. 
7. Synthesize Key Takeaways – Identify recurring themes and actionable insights rather than 
individual comments. 
8. Run Multiple Groups (if feasible) – Holding 2-3 groups with different segments of the 
market can provide more robust validation and different insights based on the group’s 
composition. 
 
Customer Discovery through Interviews 
Direct interviews with potential customers offer in-depth qualitative feedback for your invention’s 
potential impact.  
 
Here’s how to conduct effective customer interviews:

1. Identify the Right Interviewees – Choose people who represent decision-makers, end-
users, or influencers in the buying process. 
2. Structure the Interview but Stay Flexible – Prepare a list of key questions but allow room 
for organic discussion. 
3. Start Broad, Then Get Specific – Begin with general industry questions before asking about 
their specific challenges and how your invention might fit. 
4. Ask Open-Ended Questions – Encourage elaboration (e.g., "Can you describe a recent 
challenge you faced with [problem your invention solves]?"). 
5. Actively Listen and Probe Deeper – Follow up on interesting responses with "Can you tell 
me more about that?" or "Why do you think that happens?" 
6. Avoid Selling – The goal is to gather insights, not pitch your solution. 
7. Take Detailed Notes or Record (with consent) – This ensures accuracy and lets you focus 
on the conversation. 
8. Analyze Themes and Patterns – After multiple interviews, look for consistent pain points, 
unmet needs, and potential market opportunities. 
 
Secondary Research 
Secondary research leverages existing data to analyze market potential, trends, and competitive 
landscapes.  
 
Defining TAM, SAM and SOM 
Understanding market size is essential for assessing commercial viability. These three metrics 
provide a structured approach: 
1. Total Addressable Market (TAM) – The overall revenue opportunity if 100% of the market 
adopted your innovation. 
• Example: If your invention is a novel wound dressing, TAM would include all global 
wound care product sales. 
2. Serviceable Available Market (SAM) – The portion of the TAM your company could 
realistically serve based on geographic, regulatory, or industry constraints. 
• Example: If your wound dressing is only available in the U.S., your SAM is the U.S. 
wound care market. 
3. Serviceable Obtainable Market (SOM) – The fraction of SAM your company can capture, 
based on competitive positioning and go-to-market strategy. 
• Example: If your startup can realistically gain 5% of the U.S. market within five years, 
that’s your SOM. 
 
These metrics can be defined using several methods: 
1. Use industry reports – Sources like IBISWorld, Gartner, Frost & Sullivan, and Statista 
provide market size estimates.

2. Analyze government and trade association data – The Census Bureau, FDA, and industry

3. Use competitive benchmarks – Review financial reports from established companies in

Using AI Tools like ChatGPT 
AI tools like ChatGPT can streamline secondary research by summarizing vast amounts of data, 
generating insights, and identifying trends, but there are limitations. AI-generated information 
requires fact checking against reliable sources, as the data sources may be outdated, incomplete, 
or skewed. 
 
Some methods for using AI tools in market analysis include: 
1. Industry Trend Analysis - “What are the latest trends in [industry]?” AI can summarize 
reports, patents, and news to identify emerging technologies. 
2. Competitor Analysis - “Who are the key competitors in [market]?” AI can help analyze 
company strategies, funding, and innovations. 
3. Customer Pain Points - “What are common challenges faced by customers in [industry]?” 
AI can synthesize customer feedback from reviews, forums, and case studies. 
4. Generating Hypotheses for Testing - AI can help structure survey questions, interview 
scripts, and market hypotheses to validate with primary research. 
 
Keep in mind, some AI tools use information you put into their system to train models of data. 
Before you enter anything that could be considered proprietary, confirm that your AI tools meet 
required security standards for your department.

Understanding Competition 
A critical component of market analysis is understanding competition. Even if an invention is 
groundbreaking, alternative solutions, substitutes, or indirect competitors exist.  
 
What’s the worst answer you can give? "My solution doesn’t have competition." - it signals a lack of 
market awareness and a failure to understand customer alternatives.  
 
Understanding competition is part of understanding whether your work is grounded in real-world 
conditions. Investors, industry partners, and commercialization teams expect a well-researched 
competitive landscape to assess an invention’s viability, and that includes the customer’s ability to 
continue life without your invention. 
 
A competitive matrix allows you to demonstrate your research and understanding of how your 
invention fits in the market. 
 
Developing a Competitive Matrix 
A competitive matrix is a structured tool to compare key market players and position an invention 
effectively. It helps identify gaps, strengths, and differentiators. 
 
1. Identify Competitors 
• Direct competitors - same solution, same market. 
• Indirect competitors - alternative solutions that solve the same problem (including 
no solution). 
• Future competitors - companies developing similar technologies. 
2. Select Comparison Factors 
• Features & Functionality – What does each competitor offer? 
• Pricing & Business Model – How do they charge? What are margins? 
• Market Share & Customer Base – Who are their primary customers? 
• Intellectual Property & Innovation – Do they have patents or proprietary technology? 
• Regulatory Approvals – Are there legal barriers for market entry? 
• Customer Satisfaction & Reviews – What are common complaints or praises?

Defining your Market Position 
After building a competitive matrix, the next step is positioning. Rather than just being "better," an 
invention should focus on value creation. 
 
1. Avoid Competing on Price Alone - If an innovation is unique, pricing too low may signal 
low value. If competitors have lower-cost solutions, emphasize ROI and efficiency rather 
than just cost. 
2. Emphasize Unique Strengths - Identify 1-2 key differentiators and focus messaging 
around them. Examples: 
• Faster results (e.g., diagnostic tools that reduce waiting time). 
• Improved accuracy (e.g., AI-driven analytics with 98% precision). 
• More user-friendly (e.g., a no-code software alternative to complex solutions). 
3. Leverage Customer Pain Points - Study competitor negative reviews to find pain points 
customers want solved. Position the invention as an improvement in those areas. 
4. Consider Go-To-Market Advantages - Speed to market, partnerships, or regulatory fast-
tracking can be strategic advantages. Example: If competitors require expensive FDA 
approvals, a non-regulated alternative could be a major selling point. 
 
A strong competitive analysis proves market understanding and guides positioning strategy. The 
goal is not just to show where an invention fits, but also to demonstrate why it offers a compelling 
advantage over existing solutions. 
 
Building Barriers to Entry 
Barriers to entry are obstacles that make it difficult for new entrants to enter a market. 
Understanding these barriers helps inventors assess the challenges they may face in 
commercialization and develop strategies to overcome them. Barriers can be structural (inherent to 
the market) or strategic (imposed by competitors or regulations). 
 
This section outlines key barriers to entry, how they impact commercialization, and strategies for 
overcoming them.

Key Barriers to Entry 
1. Regulatory & Legal Barriers - Many industries require compliance with government 
regulations, certifications, and safety standards before a product can be sold.  
• Examples: FDA approval for medical devices, environmental and safety regulations 
for industrial products, data privacy laws (e.g., GDPR, HIPAA) for software solutions. 
• Impact: Lengthy approval processes can delay commercialization and increase 
costs. 
2. Intellectual Property (IP) & Patent Restrictions - Existing patents can block market entry if 
a similar technology is already protected. Conversely, a lack of IP protection makes an 
innovation vulnerable to imitation. 
• Examples: A competitor holds a broad patent that prevents similar innovations, your 
invention lacks patent protection, making it easy to copy. 
• Impact: Without a strong IP strategy, investors may see high legal risks. 
3. High Capital Requirements - Some industries require significant investment in R&D, 
manufacturing, equipment, or marketing before a product becomes commercially viable. 
• Examples: Biotech startups needing millions for clinical trials, semiconductor 
companies requiring expensive fabrication facilities. 
• Impact: Lack of funding can slow or halt commercialization. 
4. Market Dominance by Established Competitors - Well-established players often have 
strong brand recognition, customer loyalty, and economies of scale that make it difficult for 
new entrants to compete. 
• Examples: A startup creating a new search engine struggles against Google, a small 
medical device company competes with Johnson & Johnson. 
• Impact: Competitors can undercut pricing, block distribution channels, or use legal 
tactics to deter new entrants. 
5. Customer Adoption & Trust - Convincing customers to switch to a new, unproven solution 
can be a major hurdle. 
• Examples: Hospitals hesitant to adopt a new medical device due to liability 
concerns, B2B clients unwilling to change existing workflows for a new software 
tool. 
• Impact: Slow adoption rates can delay revenue generation. 
6. Distribution & Supply Chain Barriers - Even if an innovation is ready for market, limited 
access to distribution channels, supply chains, or retailers can restrict sales. 
• Examples: Consumer goods startups struggling to secure shelf space at major 
retailers, a food-tech company unable to meet supply chain requirements for 
nationwide distribution. 
• Impact: Limited scalability and growth potential without the right distribution 
partners. 
 
Identifying barriers to entry helps inventors prepare for challenges before entering the market.

Defining your Target Market 
A well-defined target market ensures an invention aligns with the right customer needs, increasing 
the likelihood of successful commercialization. Identifying the right market segment involves 
analyzing who the customers are, what motivates them, and how they behave. 
 
To effectively define a target market, segmentation should be approached from three perspectives: 
• Demographics (Who are they?) 
• Psychographics (Why do they buy?) 
• Behavioral Patterns (How do they engage with products?) 
 
Segmenting Customers by Demographics 
Demographic segmentation helps categorize potential customers based on measurable factors. 
This is useful for B2C (business-to-consumer) and B2B (business-to-business) markets. 
 
For B2C (Individual Customers): 
1. Age – Are they millennials, Gen X, or baby boomers? Each age group has different 
purchasing behaviors. 
2. Gender – Some innovations cater to specific genders (e.g., women’s healthcare devices). 
3. Income Level – Does the invention serve a premium or budget-conscious audience? 
4. Education Level – Highly technical products may appeal to an educated customer base. 
5. Location – Urban vs. rural, domestic vs. international markets. 
6. Occupation – Professionals, students, or retirees may have different needs. 
 
For B2B (Organizations and Companies): 
1. Industry – Healthcare, manufacturing, education, etc. 
2. Company Size – Small startups vs. large enterprises. 
3. Revenue & Budget – Can they afford premium solutions, or do they need cost-effective 
options? 
4. Geographic Reach – Local businesses vs. global corporations. 
 
Example: A smart medical diagnostic tool might target hospitals (B2B) in metropolitan areas that 
serve high-income patients (B2C) in their 50s and 60s who are proactive about health monitoring. 
 
Segmenting Customers by Psychographics 
Psychographic segmentation helps understand customer motivations, values, and lifestyle 
choices. 
1. Pain Points & Needs – What problems do they experience that the invention solves? 
2. Values & Beliefs – Are they driven by sustainability, innovation, or cost-effectiveness? 
3. Lifestyle & Interests – Do they prioritize convenience, health, or technology?

5. Risk Tolerance – Are they comfortable trying new solutions, or do they prefer proven

sustainability-driven companies that prioritize reducing their carbon footprint.

Behavioral segmentation categorizes customers based on how they interact with products and

5. Price Sensitivity – Are they bargain

high-quality solutions?

commerce platforms, and value convenience and security over price. 
 
Defining a target market is more than just identifying potential buyers—it’s about understanding 
their needs, behaviors, and decision-making processes. The strongest commercialization 
strategies combine all three segmentation approaches to refine messaging, pricing, and 
distribution channels. 
Using Market Analysis to Develop Sales Forecasts 
A well-informed sales forecast is a key component of commercialization planning. It allows 
innovators to estimate future revenue, plan production and distribution, and attract investors by 
demonstrating potential market success. A strong sales forecast leverages insights from previous 
market analysis steps, including target market segmentation, competition analysis, and barriers to 
entry.

To build a sales forecast, follow these structured steps: 
1. Define Your Assumptions - Before projecting sales, define the key assumptions that 
influence your estimates:  
• Market Penetration Rate – Based on your TAM, SAM, and SOM, estimate what 
percentage of your obtainable market you can realistically capture. 
• Customer Acquisition Rate – How quickly can you convert leads into paying 
customers? 
• Pricing Strategy – What is your expected price point per unit or service? 
• Sales Cycle – How long does it take for a customer to go from awareness to 
purchase? (e.g., enterprise sales may take months, while consumer products may 
sell instantly). 
• Seasonality & Trends – Are there expected fluctuations based on industry trends or 
economic factors? 
2. Estimate the Number of Potential Customers - Using demographic, psychographic, and 
behavioral segmentation, determine the number of potential customers in your SOM 
(Serviceable Obtainable Market).  
3. Develop a Sales Projection Model - Create a realistic sales projection by estimating the 
number of units sold or services delivered over a specific period (e.g., monthly, quarterly, or 
annually).

Note on Investor Expectations and Projections 
Investors will always discount your projections.  
When presenting a sales forecast, avoid labeling your estimates as “conservative” or “modest”—
investors assume projections are optimistic and will automatically adjust them downward based 
on risk factors, competitive pressures, and market uncertainties. 
Instead, focus on: 
• Clearly defining the assumptions behind your forecast. 
• Using data-driven justifications for your market size and growth rates. 
• Presenting multiple scenarios (optimistic, realistic, and pessimistic) to demonstrate 
awareness of market dynamics. 
Rather than underselling potential growth, provide a realistic yet compelling projection backed by 
strong market research. Investors are more likely to engage when they see a well-reasoned, data-
supported outlook rather than an artificially low "conservative" estimate. 
Summary 
Moving an innovation beyond the university requires more than just a great idea - it requires 
strategic market analysis, competitive positioning, and financial forecasting. This whitepaper 
provided a structured approach to help UMKC faculty researchers and graduate students assess 
the commercial potential of their innovations. 
Key takeaways from this framework include: 
• Market Research Foundations – Conduct primary research (surveys, focus groups, 
interviews) and leverage secondary research (industry reports, AI tools, library resources). 
• Competitive Analysis – Develop a competitive matrix to understand industry players and 
position an innovation effectively. Never assume "no competition" exists—alternative 
solutions and substitutes always influence the market. 
• Understanding Barriers to Entry – Identify regulatory, financial, and operational challenges 
that could impact commercialization and develop mitigation strategies. 
• Target Market Definition – Segment customers based on demographics, psychographics, 
and behavioral patterns to refine marketing and sales efforts. 
• Sales Forecasting – Use TAM, SAM, and SOM data to estimate revenue, account for 
industry challenges, and build realistic projections for commercialization success. 
By following this structured approach, innovators can validate their ideas, attract funding, and build 
a solid commercialization strategy that maximizes the impact of their research.
