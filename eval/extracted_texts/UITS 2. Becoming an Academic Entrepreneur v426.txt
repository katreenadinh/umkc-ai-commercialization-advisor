Understanding one pathway for moving your research into real -world use

many faculty, the deeper motivation behind that 
work is impact - improving lives, influencing 
practice, and solving real-world problems beyond 
the university. 
 
In many cases, that kind of impact requires 
translation. Academic entrepreneurship is one 
pathway for doing that. It represents a structured 
way to move research into real-world use by building 
something new around it. 
 
At UMKC, we're committed to fostering a supportive 
environment for researchers who wish to translate

applications. This guide will walk you through what it

benefits, challenges, and how UMKC can support you on this journey. 
What is Academic Entrepreneurship? 
Academic entrepreneurship is one of several pathways that can move research into real-world use. 
Others include licensing, partnerships, and adoption in practice. 
 
Academic entrepreneurship involves the commercialization of innovations developed within 
academic settings. It's about taking your research beyond the lab and into the marketplace, where 
it can make a tangible impact on society. This can involve patenting your discoveries, forming 
startups, or partnering with existing companies to develop and market new technologies. 
 
In today's fast-paced, technology-driven world, the traditional boundaries of academia are 
expanding. The internet has democratized knowledge, making it accessible to a global audience.  
 
In some cases, translating research into use allows faculty to more directly contribute to public 
understanding, practice, and application.

Challenges and How to Overcome Them 
Like any pathway to impact, academic entrepreneurship comes with tradeoffs, constraints, and 
decisions that shape how and whether it makes sense for your work. Becoming an academic 
entrepreneur presents unique challenges that differ from those faced by traditional startup 
founders.  
 
While faculty members have access to cutting-edge research and institutional resources, they 
must also navigate intellectual property policies, secure funding, and balance commercialization 
efforts with their academic responsibilities.  
 
Furthermore, venture investors often expect full-time commitment, creating a difficult decision for 
those wanting to remain in academia. Understanding these challenges in advance and developing 
strategies to overcome them can significantly improve the chances of a successful transition from 
research to commercialization. The following sections address key challenges and provide 
actionable solutions to help faculty entrepreneurs navigate this complex landscape. 
 
Intellectual Property & University Policies 
Navigating the complexities of intellectual property (IP) ownership is one of the first challenges 
faculty members face when considering commercialization. Universities often retain ownership of 
innovations developed using institutional resources. However, researchers can negotiate licensing 
rights, allowing them to commercialize their work while still adhering to university policies.  
 
Balancing academic responsibilities with entrepreneurship requires careful planning. Faculty 
members must ensure that their startup activities align with university conflict-of-interest policies 
while managing their time effectively. Transparency is key, as institutions have policies in place to 
guide faculty who are actively engaged in commercial ventures.

Protecting research through patents or other legal mechanisms before public disclosure is

Funding & Grants 
Securing funding is a critical step in turning research into a viable commercial product. Many 
academic entrepreneurs rely on a combination of grants, investments, and institutional resources 
to support their ventures.  
 
Federal programs such as Small Business Innovation Research (SBIR) and Small Business 
Technology Transfer (STTR) grants provide funding specifically for early-stage commercialization 
efforts.   
 
Additionally, UMKC offers internal funding opportunities to support innovation through programs at 
the UMKC Innovation Center including Digital Sandbox and through the Entrepreneurship 
Innovation Grant (EIG) and Regnier Venture Creation Challenge administered by the Bloch School. 
 
It is important to note that traditional research grants are typically restricted to academic purposes 
and cannot always be used for commercialization. Faculty must explore specific translational 
research grants or industry partnerships that allow for commercialization-related activities.  
 
When considering equity funding for an invention, one significant challenge is that most venture 
investors expect founders to be full-time entrepreneurs, which can be difficult for faculty members 
who wish to retain their academic roles. To address this, faculty can explore models where they 
serve as scientific advisors while bringing in an operational co-founder to manage the company. 
Alternatively, some universities offer policies that allow faculty members to take a leave of absence 
or reduce teaching loads to dedicate more time to their startup while maintaining their university 
affiliation. 
 
The UMKC Office of Commercialization provides guidance and connections to funding sources that 
support technology transfer and startup development. 
 
Time Management for Faculty Entrepreneurs 
Balancing entrepreneurship with academic responsibilities can be one of the biggest challenges for 
faculty members. Teaching, research, and administrative duties require significant time investment, 
making it difficult to focus on commercialization efforts. Faculty members interested in 
entrepreneurship should consider strategies such as reducing teaching loads, delegating 
administrative responsibilities, and setting clear boundaries between academic and startup 
commitments.

Where Academic Entrepreneurship Fits Among Translation Pathways 
There are multiple avenues for faculty members to bring their research innovations to market. 
Starting a company is one pathway; it is not always the right one 
 
Many researchers successfully commercialize their discoveries by licensing their technology to 
established companies, forming industry partnerships, or participating in joint ventures. Each 
pathway has its own set of advantages and considerations, from retaining control over the 
innovation to leveraging existing market expertise and infrastructure. Faculty members must 
evaluate these options carefully based on their goals, resources, and long-term commitment to 
commercialization.  
 
The following sections explore various commercialization strategies to help faculty determine the 
best approach for translating their research into real-world impact. 
 
Faculty-Industry Collaboration Models 
Collaborating with industry partners can accelerate the commercialization of academic research 
while providing valuable insights into real-world applications. Faculty members can engage with 
companies through sponsored research agreements, licensing deals, joint ventures, or consulting 
roles. These collaborations help bridge the gap between academia and the private sector, ensuring 
that innovations are developed in a way that meets market needs. 
 
Industry collaborations can also provide funding for research that may not qualify for traditional 
academic grants. Companies often look for university partnerships to leverage cutting-edge 
research while providing financial resources and commercial expertise. Establishing long-term 
relationships with industry stakeholders can lead to continued support for faculty-led innovations 
and facilitate knowledge exchange that benefits both academia and business. 
 
Business Formation & Strategy 
One of the key decisions in the commercialization process is whether to license technology to an 
existing company or form a startup. Licensing allows faculty members to leverage an established 
company's resources for product development and distribution, reducing the burden of building a 
business from scratch. However, starting a company offers greater control over the innovation 
process and potential long-term financial benefits.

For those choosing to launch a startup, selecting the right legal structure is crucial. The decision 
between forming a Limited Liability Company (LLC), a C-Corporation, or another entity depends on 
funding goals, ownership structure, and operational needs. Working with legal and business experts 
can help faculty entrepreneurs make informed decisions about business formation. 
 
Building a strong team is equally important. Faculty members may need to collaborate with co-
founders, business professionals, or industry experts to complement their technical expertise. 
Many successful academic startups involve partnerships between researchers and experienced 
entrepreneurs who can handle the business and operational aspects of the company. 
 
UMKC’s Office of Commercialization works with researchers to identify and connect with 
entrepreneurs who can take their research public. This allows faculty members to maintain their 
academic roles while serving as scientific advisors to the company. By leveraging this approach, 
faculty can focus on advancing research while an experienced entrepreneur leads 
commercialization efforts. 
 
Industry & Market Considerations 
Understanding market demand and industry trends is essential for successful commercialization. 
Many academic entrepreneurs face the challenge of working in isolation from potential customers, 
which can lead to misalignment between research innovations and market needs. Engaging with 
industry partners early in the process can provide insights into potential applications, regulatory 
requirements, and commercialization pathways. 
 
Conducting market research is a crucial step in validating the commercial viability of an innovation. 
This may involve customer interviews, competitive analysis, and pilot studies. Universities, 
including UMKC, offer resources such as business development workshops and mentorship 
programs to help faculty assess market potential and refine commercialization strategies. 
 
Regulatory & Compliance 
Regulatory considerations vary by industry and can significantly impact the commercialization 
timeline. Faculty members in fields such as healthcare, biotechnology, and engineering must 
navigate compliance requirements, including FDA approvals, HIPAA regulations, and environmental 
safety standards. Understanding these requirements early on can prevent costly delays in product 
development. 
 
Additionally, tax and legal considerations should not be overlooked. Faculty members who receive 
startup revenue or hold equity in a company should consult with financial and legal professionals 
to ensure compliance with tax regulations.

Exit Strategies for Faculty Entrepreneurs 
Planning an exit strategy is essential for faculty entrepreneurs who want to transition their startup to 
long-term sustainability. Exit strategies may include selling the company to a larger corporation, 
licensing the technology to an industry partner, or transitioning leadership to an external CEO while 
maintaining a scientific advisory role. 
 
Faculty members should consider their long-term goals early in the commercialization process. If 
their priority is to remain in academia, structuring the startup in a way that allows for a transition of 
leadership is crucial.  
 
The UMKC Office of Commercialization can provide guidance on structuring equity arrangements 
and licensing agreements to ensure that the intellectual property continues to generate value 
beyond the faculty member’s direct involvement. 
 
Conflict of Interest Policies for Faculty Entrepreneurs 
Navigating conflicts of interest is essential for faculty members engaged in entrepreneurial 
activities.

The University of Missouri System’s Policy on Conflict of Interest (CRR 330.015) establishes 
guidelines to ensure transparency, integrity, and compliance with university expectations. Faculty 
must proactively disclose any external interests that may intersect with their university roles to 
maintain ethical standards and institutional trust. 
 
Key Considerations for Faculty Entrepreneurs 
• Disclosure Requirements: Faculty members must disclose any financial interests, 
business affiliations, or external commitments related to their university responsibilities. 
This includes ownership in startups, consulting work, and any agreements with third parties 
that could present a conflict of interest. 
• Use of University Resources: University facilities, equipment, and confidential research 
must not be used for personal business ventures without prior approval. Faculty should 
ensure that their entrepreneurial activities remain separate from their academic 
responsibilities. 
• Balancing Roles and Responsibilities: Faculty entrepreneurs should establish clear 
distinctions between their academic and business engagements. Avoiding situations where 
personal financial interests could influence research, teaching, or university decision-
making is critical. 
• Compliance with Federal and Institutional Regulations: Faculty involved in federally 
funded research must adhere to additional conflict of interest disclosure and management 
requirements. CRR 410.020 and CRR 420.030 outline these expectations, ensuring 
compliance with national research integrity standards. 
 
By understanding and adhering to these policies, faculty entrepreneurs can successfully navigate 
potential conflicts of interest while maintaining professional integrity and institutional compliance. 
The UMKC Innovation Center and the Office of Commercialization can provide guidance on best 
practices and proper disclosure procedures.
