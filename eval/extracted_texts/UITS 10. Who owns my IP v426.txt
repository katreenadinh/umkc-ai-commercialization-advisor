"Who Owns My IP?"  
Helping you move your work beyond the university  
 
Intellectual property (IP) ownership determines who 
controls how your work can be used, developed, 
and shared beyond the university. 
 
In a university setting, that ownership can involve 
multiple stakeholders, including the institution, 
researchers, students, and funding agencies. 
 
This whitepaper aims to clarify the key aspects of IP 
ownership for faculty researchers and graduate 
students at the University of Missouri-Kansas City 
(UMKC). For more specific information about IP 
ownership, please refer to the UM System Rules & 
Regulations, Chapter 100.020 - Invention and Patent 
Regulations.  
 
Why This Matters 
As research begins to move beyond discovery and 
toward real-world use, questions about ownership become important. 
 
• Who owns the intellectual property? 
• What rights do you retain? 
• How does ownership affect what happens next? 
 
These are not just legal questions. They shape how your work can be developed, shared, and 
ultimately used outside the university. 
 
University IP Policies and Employment Contracts 
UMKC, like many universities, has clear policies regarding the ownership of inventions created 
within its environment. Typically, any invention developed using university resources or within the 
scope of an individual's employment is owned by the university. These stipulations are often 
included in the employment contracts of faculty and staff.  
 
The university's intellectual property policies further reinforce this, ensuring that the institution can 
manage and commercialize the inventions developed within its facilities. The rationale behind this 
approach is to enable the university to fulfill its obligations under various research agreements, 
including those involving federal grants.

The Bayh-Dole Act 
A significant factor in university IP ownership is the Bayh-Dole Act, a federal statute that allows 
universities to retain ownership of inventions made under federally funded research programs.  
 
This legislation requires universities to report such inventions, manage their patents, and grant 
certain rights to the federal government. This framework is crucial because it standardizes the 
management of federally funded inventions, ensuring that they are utilized for public benefit while 
allowing universities to commercialize the technology.  
 
This framework allows universities to take responsibility for moving federally funded innovations 
toward real-world use. 
 
In practice, it ensures that promising discoveries are not left unused, and that there is a structured 
path for development, partnership, and impact. 
 
Ownership for Faculty and Staff 
For faculty and staff, the ownership of inventions is generally straightforward: if an invention is 
created within the scope of their duties or using university resources, it belongs to the 
university. This policy covers inventions made during research activities, using university facilities, 
or developed with university financing.  
 
Faculty members are required to disclose their inventions to the university so that the appropriate 
processes can begin. 
 
At UMKC, two offices play distinct roles: 
• Office of Technology Transfer (OTT) manages intellectual property protection, including 
patents and licensing 
• Office of Commercialization (OC) supports the translation of research into real-world use 
through market understanding, partner engagement, and pathway development 
 
This distinction is important. Protection enables optionality. Commercialization determines how 
that work moves forward. 
 
Graduate Students and IP Ownership 
Graduate student ownership depends on how the work is conducted and supported. 
 
If a graduate student is: 
• Funded through a grant  
• Employed by the university  
• Working within a faculty-led research project  
• Using significant university resources  
 
Then the resulting intellectual property is typically owned by the university.

If a student develops work independently, outside of employment and without significant use of 
university resources, they may retain ownership. 
 
However, these distinctions are often not obvious. Many graduate research activities are tied to 
funding, lab infrastructure, and faculty direction. 
 
When in doubt, it is important to clarify ownership early. This avoids confusion later, especially if 
the work begins to show potential for real-world use. 
 
Undergraduate Students 
Undergraduate students generally retain ownership of their work unless: 
• They are employed by the university  
• They are contributing to funded research  
• They are using significant university-controlled resources beyond normal coursework  
 
Most class projects and student-led work remain student-owned. However, once a project 
becomes part of formal research activity, ownership may shift. 
 
Collaborative and Sponsored Research 
Collaborative research, often involving multiple inventors from different institutions or companies, 
presents unique challenges in IP ownership. Clear agreements must be in place to define the rights 
of each party. The university's commercialization team assists in negotiating these agreements, 
ensuring that all contributors' rights are respected, and that the commercialization process can 
proceed smoothly.  
 
Sponsored research, funded by external companies or organizations, also has specific IP terms. 
Generally, the university retains ownership but may grant licenses to the sponsor.  
 
Understanding these terms early is critical. 
 
Ownership and rights determine whether a partner can adopt, license, or invest in the work. 
Without clear agreements, even strong innovations can stall before reaching use. 
 
Dispute Resolution and Waivers 
Disputes over IP ownership can arise, particularly in complex collaborative environments.  
 
UMKC has formal review processes to address questions of intellectual property ownership when 
they arise, typically involving the Office of Technology Transfer and university legal counsel.  
 
Inventors who believe their invention falls outside the university’s claim can request a waiver or 
review. This process ensures that all parties have a fair opportunity to present their case and reach 
an equitable resolution.

In some cases, the university may decide not to pursue intellectual property protection. When this 
happens, ownership may be released to the inventor through a formal waiver process. 
 
This typically occurs when: 
• The commercial opportunity is limited  
• Patent protection is unlikely  
• The cost of protection outweighs 
potential value  
 
If ownership is waived, the inventor assumes full 
responsibility for: 
• Patent costs  
• Development expenses  
• Use of facilities and resources  
 
At that point, the work moves outside the 
university system and must be advanced 
independently or through external support. 
 
Conclusion 
Ownership is not just a legal detail. It is a structural factor that determines how your work can move 
forward. 
 
At UMKC, ownership policies are designed to: 
• Enable protection of promising work  
• Create pathways for development and use  
• Ensure compliance with funding requirements  
 
Understanding these policies helps you make informed decisions about when to disclose, how to 
engage, and what options are available. 
 
If your work has the potential to create impact beyond the university, ownership is one of the first 
pieces that needs to be clear. 
 
For specific questions or further clarification, contacting the UMKC Office of Technology Transfer is 
recommended.
