Startup or License? Finding the Right Path for Your 
Innovation 
A Whitepaper for UMKC Faculty Researchers and Graduate Students  
Research at UMKC often begins with discovery, but 
for many faculty and graduate researchers, the 
deeper goal is impact. Some innovations reach that 
impact through adoption in practice, partnerships 
with existing organizations, licensing, or the creation 
of a startup.  
 
In the U.S., frameworks like the Bayh-Dole Act 
enable universities to retain ownership of inventions 
and support pathways that move research into use. 
At UMKC, this framework supports the university’s 
strategic goals of increasing research impact, 
fostering entrepreneurship, and building strong 
industry partnerships. 
 
When a new invention is disclosed, one of the first 
decisions the researcher, the Office of 
Commercialization, and the Office of Technology 
Transfer must make is whether to pursue a startup 
venture or license the technology to an existing company.  
 
This whitepaper focuses on one important decision point within that broader translation process: 
whether your innovation is better suited for licensing to an existing company or for development 
through a new venture. 
Two Pathways to Impact 
Once your innovation is disclosed, one of the most important strategic decisions is choosing the 
best path to bring it to market: licensing the technology to an existing company or launching a new 
startup. Both routes can lead to successful commercialization and public impact, but they require 
different levels of involvement, risk, and support.  
 
Not every innovation needs to become a startup, and not every project is best advanced through a 
license. These are two important pathways, but the right choice depends on the readiness of the 
work, the market context, and the role you want to play.

This section outlines each path in detail - highlighting when it works best, what benefits it brings,

Licensing to an Existing Company 
Licensing is often the best fit when there is a clear market need, a company already has the 
capability to deploy the innovation, and the researcher prefers a more limited ongoing role in 
commercialization activity. Licensing involves granting an established company the rights to use, 
develop, and commercialize a university-owned technology under defined terms. 
 
Best For: 
• Technologies that are close to market-ready 
• Established markets with well-defined customer needs 
• Inventors who prefer to focus on research 
Benefits: 
• Less risk for the inventor and university 
• Company takes on product development and market entry 
• Potential for revenue through royalties or milestone payments 
 
UMKC Example: Evolution Pervious licensed technology from UMKC to commercialize a novel 
material that improves wastewater collection and helps reduce PFAS contamination. The license 
allowed the company to further develop and deploy the technology while UMKC retained rights and 
potential financial return. 
 
Launching a Startup 
Startups are often most appropriate when the innovation is still early, the path to adoption needs to 
be built, and the work requires sustained development that an existing company is unlikely to take 
on immediately. Forming a startup involves licensing the innovation to a newly created company, 
often led or supported by the inventors themselves. 
 
Best For: 
• Disruptive or early-stage technologies 
• Innovations requiring further technical and market development 
• Inventors interested in entrepreneurship and long-term involvement 
Benefits: 
• Greater long-term upside, with higher uncertainty and responsibility 
• More control over product development and direction 
• Potential to build new capabilities, teams, or markets around the innovation 
 
UMKC Example: EyeVerify (later Zoloz) was born from biometric research at UMKC. The company 
developed eye-based authentication for mobile devices and grew rapidly with support from grants

Key Questions to Guide Your Decision 
Deciding whether to license your innovation or build a startup isn’t just about preference - it’s about 
alignment. The right commercialization path depends on the readiness of your technology, the state 
of the market, and your personal and professional goals.  
 
The goal is not to choose the more exciting option, but to identify which path best aligns with your 
work. Asking the right questions early can help clarify the best way forward and avoid costly 
detours.

innovation to the path most likely to lead to real-world impact.

What Role Do You Want to Play? 
• Do you want to lead a company or stay focused on research? 
• Are you energized by the challenge of creating something new—or more interested in seeing 
it scaled by an existing firm? 
• Are you comfortable with financial risk, team-building, and customer engagement? 
 
Answering these questions honestly can help guide your next step. 
UMKC Support for Both Paths 
The decision to launch a startup or pursue licensing should begin with a realistic assessment of 
your technology, your market, and your personal goals as a researcher. Ask yourself: Do I want to be 
actively involved in a company? Is my technology ready for market, or does it need years of 
development? Is there an existing company eager to take this on or do I need to create one?

UMKC supports this process through two complementary roles. The Office of Technology Transfer 
focuses on intellectual property, including protection, ownership, and licensing structures. The 
Office of Commercialization focuses on how innovations move into real-world use, including

Together, these teams provide coordinated guidance to help you move from idea to impact with 
clarity and confidence.

Decision Framework: A Simple Flow 
With so many variables to consider, deciding 
between a startup and licensing can feel 
overwhelming. But the right path often becomes 
clear when you focus on a few core questions 
about your technology, your market, and your 
goals.  
 
This simple decision framework is designed to 
help you quickly assess which option aligns best 
with your situation. While not a substitute for 
expert guidance, it’s a helpful starting point for 
productive conversations with UMKC’s Office of 
Commercialization and Office of Technology 
Transfer. 
 
Ask yourself: 
• Do I want to actively lead or support a business? 
• Is the market for this innovation already clear? 
• How much work remains before this technology is usable? 
Guiding Question: 
Which pathway gives your 
work the strongest chance 
of reaching real-world use 
given its current stage, 
market context, and the role 
you wish to play?

• Do I have (or want to build) a team with business expertise? 
 
General Rule of Thumb: 
• If you answer "Yes" to market clarity and "No" to entrepreneurial interest → License 
• If you answer "Yes" to founding a company and "Yes" to ongoing development needs → 
Startup 
 
Note: Some innovators may explore both options over time. For example, a technology might be 
licensed early for pilot deployment and later become the foundation for a startup. Your 
commercialization journey is not always linear. 
 
Make this decision in consultation with the Office of Commercialization and the Office of 
Technology Transfer, who will work with you to assess readiness, risk, and resource needs. 
Suggested Steps for UMKC Innovators 
Once you’ve identified the likely path for your innovation - licensing or startup - the next step is to 
take action. UMKC offers a clear and supportive process to help you move forward, from disclosing 
your invention to building a commercialization plan.  
 
These recommended steps will connect you with the right people, tools, and resources to maximize 
your innovation’s potential and turn your ideas into real-world solutions. 
1. Submit an Innovation Disclosure: Early disclosure allows for better evaluation of market 
pathways. 
2. Schedule a Commercialization Meeting: Align on goals, technology readiness, and 
inventor interest. 
3. Evaluate the Market: Use internal and external tools to understand competition, 
customers, and barriers. 
4. Clarify Inventor Goals: Decide how involved you want to be. 
5. Leverage UMKC Resources: Use funding, mentorship, and market development tools to 
strengthen your approach. 
Conclusion 
Licensing and startup formation are not competing goals. They are different pathways through 
which research can reach real-world use. The right choice depends on the stage of the work, the 
nature of the market, and the role you want to play. What matters most is not which path appears 
more exciting, but which path gives your work the best chance to create meaningful impact beyond 
the university. 
 
At UMKC, the Office of Commercialization and the Office of Technology Transfer work in close 
collaboration with faculty and graduate researchers to explore both pathways and match the right 
resources to each project. Whether you aim to partner with industry or lead your own venture, 
UMKC is here to support you every step of the way.
