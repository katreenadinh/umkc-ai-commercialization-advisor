UMKC Guide to Licensing University IP for Startups

Purpose of This Guide

This document is intended to help founders, entrepreneurs, and startup teams understand how the University of Missouri Kansas City (UMKC) approaches licensing university-owned intellectual property (IP).

Our goal is to provide clarity on how license agreements are structured, where flexibility exists, and how we work with startups to create commercially viable companies built on UMKC technology.

This guide does not replace legal advice. Founders should engage their own attorneys when reviewing and negotiating any agreement. References to sections of the license agreement are provided for convenience only and do not modify or interpret the agreement.

How to Think About a UMKC License Agreement

A UMKC license agreement is not a blank-slate negotiation. It is a structured framework with:

Core terms that are standardized due to legal, policy, and institutional requirements

Business terms that can be structured to align with the needs of a startup

Understanding the difference between these categories will reduce negotiation time and help focus discussions on the areas that matter most.

What a License Grants (in practical terms)

A UMKC startup license typically provides:

Exclusive rights to develop and commercialize the technology within a defined field of use and territory

The ability to sublicense those rights to partners, subject to University approval

Access to the underlying intellectual property as it evolves, subject to the terms of the agreement

In practice, startup development paths often evolve. These rights are paired with obligations related to development progress, reporting, and financial participation, and companies are expected to maintain reasonable progress toward commercialization within their defined field of use.

If development plans change, companies are encouraged to engage with the Office of Commercialization early. In many cases, agreements can be adjusted to reflect updated plans where there is a clear and credible path forward.

If reasonable progress is not maintained, the University may modify exclusivity or terminate the agreement in accordance with its terms.

Terms That Are Fixed

As a public research university operating within the UM System, certain elements are not typically negotiable within UM System agreements. These terms are handled consistently across agreements, subject to applicable federal law, institutional policy, and risk management requirements.

Research and publication rights

Defined in Section 2

UMKC retains the right to use licensed technology for research, teaching, and academic purposes and to allow other non-profit entities to use the licensed technology for non-commercial research purposes (2.04)

The University maintains the right to publish research results

Indemnification, insurance, and liability structure

Defined in Article VI

Licensees must indemnify the University and carry appropriate insurance

The University provides the licensed technology “as is” and does not make representations or warranties regarding performance, commercial viability, or non-infringement

Agreements include standard limitations of liability and warranty disclaimers, including exclusion of consequential damages

As a public institution, the University operates under sovereign immunity and does not waive those protections

Government rights when applicable

Reflected in Section 2 and related provisions

May include government-use rights, march-in rights, and U.S. manufacturing requirements

These are required under federal law or policy and are not typically modified

Reporting and compliance requirements

Defined in Article IV

Licensees must report on development progress, commercialization, and revenue

Core sublicensing framework

Defined in Section 2.03

Requires University visibility and approval

Ensures obligations flow down to sublicensees

These elements are consistent across agreements and tied to legal and policy constraints.

Where We Can Be Flexible

While the legal framework is largely fixed, the business terms of a license agreement can be structured to align with a startup’s stage, capital position, and commercialization strategy.

These elements function as “levers.” They are typically not optimized independently. Instead, they are balanced against each other based on the specifics of the opportunity.

NOTE: The examples below are illustrative and depend on the specific facts of each opportunity and internal approvals.

Financial Structure (Article III)

Includes:

Running royalty rate Section 3.01 to include royalty payment from sublicensee in 3.02(a)

Upfront or license execution fee Section 3.01(a)

Minimum annual royalties Section 3.01(d)

Milestone payments Section 3.01(e)

Sublicense revenue sharing Section 3.02 (b)

Success fee Section 3.03

How this is typically structured:

Early-stage companies with limited capital often have lower upfront costs and back-loaded economics

More validated technologies or later-stage companies may support higher upfront payments or earlier financial commitments

Royalty rates are influenced by risk, market size, and how much additional development is required

Sublicense revenue sharing is influenced by whether the business model depends on partnerships or downstream licensing and is designed to align incentives between the company and the University while supporting partnership-driven growth

UMKC’s approach may include alternative structures such as success-based participation or equity depending on the opportunity

In most cases, these elements are adjusted together. For example, reducing upfront cost may result in higher downstream participation.

Timing of Economic Obligations

Includes:

When payments begin

When milestones are triggered

How obligations align with financing and revenue

How this is typically structured:

For early-stage startups, economic obligations are often deferred until key inflection points such as financing or first commercial sale

For companies closer to market, obligations may begin earlier

Milestones are ideally tied to meaningful business events rather than arbitrary dates

This is often an important lever in startup licensing. Adjusting timing can significantly reduce early pressure without reducing overall economic value.

Scope of Exclusivity

Includes:

Field of use and territory Section 2.01

How this is typically structured:

Narrower fields of use or territories may allow for more favorable economic terms

Broader exclusivity typically requires stronger diligence commitments and more robust financial structure

Exclusivity is granted based on the company’s ability to execute within the defined scope

In practice, this lever is often used to balance risk. The broader the rights granted, the higher the expectation of performance.

Diligence and Commercialization Milestones

Includes:

Development timelines

Commercialization expectations

Revenue or deployment targets

Defined in Article IV and diligence provisions

How this is typically structured:

Milestones are aligned with realistic technical and market development timelines

Early-stage companies may have longer timelines with defined checkpoints

More mature opportunities may require faster progression to market

Diligence provisions are used to ensure forward progress, not to create artificial pressure. It also provides a mechanism to adjust exclusivity if progress is not achieved.

Patent Expense Handling

Includes:

Responsibility for patent prosecution and maintenance costs defined in Section 7 and patent ownership and process control

How this is typically structured:

Licensees are typically responsible for patent costs due to limited University resources

In certain cases (and subject to internal approval), the timing of reimbursement may be adjusted including potential deferral for a defined period, typically not exceeding two years, or until financing or commercialization milestones are met

For better-capitalized companies, reimbursement may begin immediately

This is often a practical constraint rather than a negotiating position. Timing adjustments are common, but the underlying obligation is generally expected.

Key Principle

These “levers” are not used in isolation. The University’s participation reflects its role in developing and providing access to the underlying intellectual property, and terms are structured based on:

Stage of the technology

Capital required to commercialize

Time to revenue

Strength of the team

Commercial model and market opportunity

The goal is to create a structure that supports company success while maintaining a commercially reasonable return on University IP.

In most cases, this means aligning  the approximate timing of economic obligations to occur when the company has the resources to meet them.

How Economics Are Determined

Industry data, including AUTM surveys, provides general benchmarks for license terms. However, no single number applies across all deals. These benchmarks provide general context and do not determine specific deal terms or outcomes.

Where a specific agreement lands depends on:

Stage of technology development

Strength and breadth of the IP

Capital required to commercialize

Time to market and revenue

Size and clarity of the commercial opportunity

Experience and track record of the team

Commercialization model, including use of partnerships or sublicensing

As a general principle: Earlier-stage, higher-risk opportunities are typically structured to defer economic burden but not eliminate it.

Example of Startup-Aligned Structuring

The same underlying economics can be structured differently depending on company stage.

Standard structure

Startup-aligned structure

Patent costs reimbursed as incurred

Patent costs deferred until financing, first commercial sale, or a defined period (typically not exceeding two years)

Milestone payment triggered at a single threshold

Milestone payments split across multiple thresholds

Minimum annual royalties begin on a fixed timeline

Minimum annual royalties tied to commercialization milestones rather than calendar years

In both cases, the total economic value to the University may be similar. The difference is in how obligations are sequenced to support company growth.

What We Look for in a Startup Partner

We are able to move faster and be more flexible when we have confidence in a company’s ability to execute. These factors help inform how agreements are structured across opportunities.

Founders can improve the speed and outcome of the licensing process by demonstrating:

Clear commercialization plan

Defined customer and use case

Path to market

Development roadmap

Key technical and commercial milestones

6-to-18-month execution plan

Capital strategy

Expected funding sources and timing

Alignment between funding and development milestones

Team capability

Relevant technical and business experience

Clear leadership and accountability

Partnership or sublicensing strategy

Whether the model depends on partnerships

Expected structure of those relationships

Engagement with the technology

Understanding of the IP

Awareness of risks and limitations

The more clearly these elements are defined, the more effectively we can structure terms that align with the company’s needs.

How to Move Efficiently Toward an Agreement

To streamline the licensing process, early discussions should focus on:

Commercialization plan

Development timeline and key risks

Capital strategy

Alignment on these elements allows us to:

Structure appropriate economic terms

Avoid unnecessary negotiation cycles

Move more quickly toward execution

Final Note

UMKC’s goal is to enable the successful translation of university research into real-world impact through startup formation and industry engagement.

License agreements are a tool to support that outcome. The most effective agreements:

Provide startups with flexibility to grow

Maintain accountability for commercialization

Ensure a fair and reasonable return while enabling successful commercialization
