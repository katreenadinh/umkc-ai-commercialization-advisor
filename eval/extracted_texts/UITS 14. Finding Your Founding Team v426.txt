Finding Your Founding Team 
Helping you move your work beyond the university  
 
Forming the right team is one of the most important 
early decisions when work begins to move beyond 
the university into real-world use. This whitepaper 
distills best practices from both national startup 
ecosystems and UMKC’s own experience to help 
faculty and graduate students identify, recruit, and 
structure an effective founding team.  
 
Readers will learn (1) which core roles matter most 
at the very beginning, (2) how to balance skills, 
motivation, and culture, and (3) pragmatic steps to 
move from idea toward a structured effort capable 
of reaching real-world use.. 
 
Introduction 
A brilliant invention rarely becomes a successful 
company without a complementary team behind it.  
 
At UMKC, we regularly see that commercialization 
efforts accelerate when researchers pair their technical insight with partners who excel in market 
discovery, product development, and early-stage business building.  
 
Yet many university-based founders struggle to know who they actually need on day one—and how 
to persuade talented colleagues to join a venture that is still more promise than paycheck. This 
guide answers those questions and offers UMKC-specific resources to support your search. 
 
Core Roles for an Early-Stage Academic Startup 
Not all work requires forming a startup team. Some innovations move forward through licensing, 
partnerships, or adoption within existing organizations. However, when the path involves building 
something new, team formation becomes a central factor in whether that effort succeeds. 
 
Building a company out of a research breakthrough is equal parts science, story, and hustle. In the 
frenzy of grant deadlines and publication pressures, faculty founders often default to a 
lone-inventor model - only to discover that investors and industry partners evaluate ventures first 
on team completeness, second on technology maturity.

Evidence from the Kauffman Foundation and Startup Genome shows that balanced founding 
teams raise 30–40 % more capital and reach product-market fit twice as fast as solo founders. 
 
The lesson is clear: your first three seats can either multiply or mute the value of your IP. Instead of 
hiring a full C-suite, focus on covering perspectives that mirror the design-thinking lens - 
desirability, viability, and feasibility.  
 
At UMKC we frame these perspectives as the Problem Expert, Business Developer, and Technology 
Developer. One person may initially wear two hats, but explicit ownership of each lens keeps 
priorities aligned and prevents critical blind spots. 
1. Problem Expert (Visionary / Idealist) – lives and breathes the user problem, drives 
customer discovery, and keeps the venture anchored in unmet need. 
2. Business Developer – designs the business model, validates willingness-to-pay, secures 
early funding, and builds strategic partnerships. 
3. Technology Developer – turns lab-scale insights into a functional, scalable prototype 
under tight resource constraints. 
 
These roles give a startup just enough “3-legged stool” stability to navigate uncertainty without 
premature hierarchy. 
 
UMKC Insight: In past faculty/student‐founded companies such as EyeVerify, founders reported 
that aligning on these three roles early prevented later confusion about who owned regulatory 
strategy, grant writing, and product iterations. 
 
Deciding Who Will Leave the Lab 
Leaving the security of an academic appointment for a fledgling company can feel risky, 
but someone must step out of the lab and into the market full‐time if the venture is to gain 
momentum. This section lays out a structured approach for deciding who that person (or people) 
should be and how to make the leap with minimal friction. 
 
Why Someone Must Go All‐In 
Startups move at a tempo universities cannot match. Customer validation, prototype iterations, 
and investor conversations often happen in cycles measured in days—not semesters.  
 
A founder who is 100 % dedicated can: 
• Shorten feedback loops by talking with users, suppliers, and regulators daily. 
• Signal commitment to investors, partners, and future hires. 
• Catch issues early (e.g., manufacturing glitches, reimbursement hurdles) before they derail 
project timelines. 
 
UMKC Insight: Many seed funds will not invest until at least one technically knowledgeable founder 
has left the university and shows "skin in the game."

Employment & Leave Pathways at UMKC 
UMKC offers multiple mechanisms to reduce personal and departmental risk: 
• Entrepreneurial Leave – Up to one year of unpaid leave while retaining tenure clock status. 
Departments often allow a part‐time return if the venture fails. 
• Reduced Appointment (0.50–0.75 FTE) – Faculty retain benefits while freeing critical 
weekdays for startup duties. 
• Joint Appointment – Split time between UMKC and the startup; common when grant 
funding still flows through the university. 
• Graduate‐Student Fellows – Grad students may dedicate a semester via internship credit 
or a paid fellowship funded by an SBIR supplement. 
 
Contact OTT and OC early to navigate paperwork and conflict‐of‐interest disclosures. 
 
Funding Bridges for Early Salaries 
Leaving the lab is easier when initial salary support is lined up: 
• Phase I SBIR/STTR Grants – Allow up to $264 K (NIH) in technical salary and consultant 
costs. 
• FFAR Seeding Solutions or DoD–xTech Prizes – Non‐dilutive cash that can fund 
6‐12 months of runway. 
• Founders’ Agreements – Clarify whether university IP licensing fees are deferred to 
preserve cash. 
 
Handling IP & Conflict‐of‐Interest (COI) 
The founder‐as‐employee dynamic introduces potential COI concerns. Mitigate by: 
• Drafting a Management Plan with the Office of Technology Transfer that specifies roles, 
reporting lines, and disclosure cadence. 
• Separating lab resources – Use off‐campus servers, tooling, and grant accounts for 
company work.

Timing the Spin‐Out 
A practical rule: spin out once you can explain your value proposition in one sentence and show a 
benchtop demo that proves feasibility. Waiting for the “perfect” dataset often means missing the 
market window. On‐campus prototyping resources (e.g., the School of Science and Engineering 
Machine Shop or the Innovation Studio’s 3‐D printing facilities) can bridge capability gaps during 
the handoff. 
 
With a clear readiness assessment, structured UMKC pathways, and early funding options, 
founders can commit fully and confidently to building outside the university walls. 
 
Motivating and Attracting Co-Founders 
Finding co-founders is less about posting a “help wanted” ad and more about telling an irresistible 
story of impact and shared upside. High-caliber partners can work anywhere; they join startups 
that satisfy their drive for autonomy, mastery, and purpose.  
 
Below is a structured approach to locating—and winning—the right people. 
 
Clarify the Value Proposition for Co-Founders 
Before you recruit, articulate: 
1. Mission Impact: What societal or industry pain will you relieve? (e.g., "reduce hospital 
readmissions by 20 % in five years"). 
2. Unique Advantage: Why is this team uniquely positioned to solve it? Highlight your IP, data 
sets, or early validation. 
3. Upside & Timeline: Provide a realistic five-year outlook: milestone-based valuation targets, 
grant pathways, and liquidity scenarios. 
UMKC Tip: Draft a one-page “Founder Pitch” that distills these points. It becomes the leave-behind 
for coffee chats and conference follow-ups.

Crafting the Outreach 
Craft an outreach strategy that instantly communicates why your venture matters, what you need, 
and how a potential co-founder can share in the upside—this first impression should spark 
curiosity and action. 
• Role Brief: ½-page description detailing problem space, expected weekly commitment, 
and equity range (e.g., 10-20 % vesting over four years). 
• Story Hook: Lead with the mission, not the technology. “Help 300 M diabetics avoid 
blindness” moves hearts more than “utilize ML for ophthalmic imaging.” 
• Social Proof: Mention advisors, early grants (e.g., $20 K Digital Sandbox), or pilot partners 
to de-risk the opportunity. 
 
Evaluating Fit Beyond the Résumé 
Résumé screening narrows the field, but true alignment emerges only when you probe values, 
adaptability, and collaboration style through structured, real-world interactions. 
• Competency Matrix: Rate each candidate (1-5) on technical depth, market acumen, 
fund-raising comfort, and network reach. 
• Culture Interviews: Ask scenario-based questions (“Tell me about a time data disproved 
your hypothesis—what did you do?”) to probe flexibility and ego control. 
• Trial Project: Engage finalists in a two-week sprint—customer calls, prototype tweak, or 
NSF I-Corps assignment. Observe follow-through and communication cadence. 
 
Incentive Structures That Work 
Motivation compounds when compensation reflects contribution; combine equity, modest cash, 
and professional-growth perks to keep co-founders committed through the roller-coaster of early 
execution. 
• Equity with Milestone Vesting: Allocate additional slices when key targets (e.g., FDA 
510(k) submission) are met.

Building a Trustworthy & Diverse Team Culture 
Early‐stage startups succeed or fail on the quality of their relationships. Trust fuels fast iteration, 
honest customer feedback, and the stamina to survive inevitable pivots.  
 
Research from Google’s Project Aristotle and MIT’s Human Dynamics Lab shows that teams with 
high psychological safety and equal conversational turn‐taking outperform their peers in both 
execution speed and creativity. 
 
A practical trust‐building playbook for UMKC founders 
1. Start with a micro‐commitment: Before anyone signs incorporation documents, work 
together on a time‐boxed experiment—e.g., two weeks of customer interviews or a 
pitch‐deck sprint. Observe reliability and communication styles under light pressure. 
2. Establish shared values early: Draft a one‐page “Team Charter” that lists why the venture 
matters, how decisions are made, and what behaviors are non‐negotiable (e.g., data 
privacy, respect for time). Revisit quarterly. 
3. Create psychological safety: Encourage dissent by rotating the “devil’s advocate” role in 
meetings and explicitly praising well‐argued counterpoints. This normalizes productive 
disagreement and prevents groupthink.

4. Default to transparency: Maintain a living project board (e.g., Trello, Asana) that makes 
goals, blockers, and responsibilities visible to everyone. Use weekly retros to surface 
concerns before they fester. 
5. Resolve conflicts quickly: Adopt a lightweight dispute‐resolution process—start with 
one‐on‐one discussion, escalate to a neutral mentor (UMKC Innovation Center offers 
volunteer advisors), and document any decisions that affect equity or IP. 
6. Celebrate small wins: Shared rituals—demo days, customer‐interview milestones, 
monthly lunches—reinforce camaraderie and buffer stress. 
7. Leverage diversity as an asset: Founding teams with varied domains, genders, and cultural 
backgrounds generate 30–50 % more patents and venture returns. When recruiting, set 
explicit diversity goals and tap UMKC networks like Women in STEM, the Bloch School 
Entrepreneurship Scholars, and KCSourceLink’s Multicultural Business Coalition. 
 
By institutionalizing these practices, founders transform trust from a vague ideal into a measurable 
advantage. 
 
Structuring Equity & Responsibilities 
Getting equity right early can prevent costly disputes later. The goal is to align incentives with future 
value creation, protect the company if someone departs, and leave head-room for future hires and 
investors. 
 
Guiding Principles 
• Contribution over credentials: Reward the work founders will do, not just past titles or 
academic rank. 
• Simplicity beats precision: An easily understood split that everyone feels is “fair enough” 
beats a mathematically perfect but convoluted cap table. 
• Protect the mission: Use vesting and governance safeguards so a founder who disengages 
cannot block financing or pivots.

Vesting Mechanics 
Equity is a promise of future value; vesting turns that promise into earned ownership by tying shares 
to ongoing contribution and time in the trenches. The frameworks below outline standard 
schedules and investor-friendly safeguards. 
• Standard Vesting: 4-year vest with 1-year cliff; monthly thereafter. 
• Reverse Vesting: Founders purchase shares up-front but the company can repurchase 
unvested shares at nominal cost if they leave. 
• Acceleration Clauses: Single-trigger (change of control) or double-trigger (change of 
control + termination) acceleration helps founders in an acquisition but can deter investors 
- use sparingly. 
 
Founder Roles & Decision Rights 
Agree early on who is CEO (single point for investors) and how major decisions require consent: 
• Day-to-Day Authority: The CEO manages budget and hiring; CSO leads R&D road-map; 
COO handles operations. 
• Reserved Matters: Any new share issuance, IP sale, or debt financing requires unanimous 
founder approval until Series A. 
• Board Composition: Start with a three-member board (two founders + one independent 
advisor) to add external perspective without losing control. 
 
Setting Up an Option Pool 
Allocate 10–15 % of total shares for future employees, advisors, and the scientific advisory board. 
Option pools usually sit above founder ownership in pre-money valuation calculations, so plan the 
pool size before negotiating with investors. 
 
Legal Steps at UMKC 
Even the fairest equity split can unravel without airtight paperwork; follow these campus-friendly 
legal checkpoints to lock in ownership, minimize tax surprises, and stay investor-ready. 
1. Stock Purchase Agreements & 83(b) Election: File within 30 days of share issuance to 
avoid adverse tax treatment. 
2. Founder Service Agreements: Spell out roles, time commitment, and IP assignment. 
3. Local Clinics & Resources: UMKC School of Law Entrepreneur Clinic offers free reviews, 
and KCSourceLink’s Resource Navigator lists vetted startup attorneys who offer fixed-fee 
formation packages.

Conclusion 
The people involved in moving your work forward will shape whether it reaches meaningful impact 
beyond the university.  
 
By intentionally balancing expertise, motivation, equity, and by drawing on UMKC’s growing 
commercialization ecosystem you can transform lab insights into market-ready innovations faster 
and with less friction. 
 
Key Takeaways 
• Prioritize people, not positions. Anchor around complementary mindsets—Problem 
Expert, Technology Developer, and Business Developer—before chasing formal titles. 
• Commit to trust and transparency. Psychological safety, clear communication, and fast 
conflict resolution outlast any single grant or prototype. 
• Design future-focused incentives. Simple equity splits, four-year vesting, and an option 
pool keep founders, early hires, and investors rowing in the same direction.
