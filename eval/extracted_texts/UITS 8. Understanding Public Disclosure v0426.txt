Understanding Public Disclosure and Its Impact on IP Protection 
Helping you move your work beyond the university  
 
Let’s say you’ve made a breakthrough in your 
research - something innovative, potentially 
impactful. Your instinct might be to share it widely: 
present at a conference, post online, or publish in a 
journal. 
That instinct is part of academic work. But if your 
goal is for that work to create impact beyond the 
university, timing matters. 
Sharing too early or without the right protections in 
place can limit your ability to develop, protect, and 
ultimately move your work into real-world use. This 
is where understanding public disclosure becomes 
critical. 
This whitepaper is designed to help UMKC 
researchers and graduate students understand what 
counts as public disclosure, how it affects your 
ability to protect intellectual property (IP), and what you can do to avoid unintended 
consequences—without compromising your academic goals. 
What Is Public Disclosure? 
Public disclosure is any non-confidential communication of an idea, invention, or discovery to 
others. It doesn’t have to be formal or even intentional. If the information is made available to 
people who are not under a duty of confidentiality, it likely qualifies as public disclosure. 
Common Forms of Public Disclosure 
• Journal articles or conference papers (including preprints) 
• Oral presentations or poster sessions 
• Theses and dissertations once available in UMKC libraries or online repositories 
• Social media posts or content shared on personal websites 
• Product demos, showcases, or pitch competitions open to the public 
• Conversations with external collaborators if not under a non-disclosure agreement 
Tip: If you’re about to tell someone about your invention, ask yourself - “Do they have an obligation 
to keep this confidential?” If not, it’s a public disclosure.

Why Does It Matter? 
Public disclosure can dramatically affect your ability to secure patent protection. Patent law, 
especially outside the United States, places a high premium on novelty, which is lost once an 
invention becomes public. 
Key Impacts on IP Protection 
• Loss of Novelty: Once disclosed publicly, your invention is no longer considered "new" 
under patent law. 
• Grace Period in the U.S.: The U.S. gives you 12 months from the date of first public 
disclosure to file a patent application. Miss this window, and you lose your rights. 
• No Grace Period Internationally: Most other countries such as those in Europe and Asia 
follow an absolute novelty rule. Even one public mention before filing can make your 
invention unpatentable. 
Example: A UMKC grad student presented her novel sensor technology at a national conference. 
She hadn’t yet filed a provisional patent. Six months later, her international patent application was 
rejected because the presentation counted as public disclosure. 
How to Protect Your Work 
Protecting your work is not about restricting dissemination. It is about sequencing your actions so 
that you can both share your research and preserve its potential for real-world use. 
Here’s how UMKC researchers can proactively manage disclosures: 
1. File a Provisional Patent First - A provisional application is a low-cost way to establish an 
early filing date and protect your invention before you go public. It gives you 12 months to 
refine your invention and file a full (non-provisional) patent. 
2. Use Non-Disclosure Agreements (NDAs) - Before speaking with potential partners, 
companies, or even external collaborators, use an NDA to ensure confidentiality. The Office 
of Commercialization can help you prepare one. 
3. Control the Content of Public Talks - You don’t have to avoid public speaking; just be 
strategic. Share general concepts or high-level benefits, but avoid diving into technical 
details or enabling someone to replicate your work. 
4. Request an Embargo on Your Thesis - UMKC allows students to delay the public release of 
a thesis or dissertation. This gives you time to secure patent protection before your work 
becomes accessible through university repositories. 
5. Consult Early and Often - Before submitting a manuscript, preparing a pitch deck, or 
sharing slides at a conference, check in with the UMKC Office of Technology Transfer. We’re 
here to help you find the best path forward.

Public Disclosure vs. Confidential Communication 
Understanding whether a particular activity constitutes a public disclosure can sometimes feel 
murky, especially when academic work often involves collaboration, presentations, and 
publication. The key distinction lies in who has access to the information and whether a 
confidentiality agreement is in place.  
The table below offers a quick guide to help you determine which scenarios are considered public 
disclosures (and could jeopardize patent rights) versus those that remain confidential and safe 
from an intellectual property standpoint. 
Scenario Disclosure Type 
Journal article published online Public Disclosure 
Internal group meeting at UMKC Non-Disclosure 
Presentation at a public conference Public Disclosure 
Email to a collaborator without NDA Public Disclosure 
Email to a collaborator with NDA Non-Disclosure 
Thesis available in UMKC's digital repository Public Disclosure 
Thesis under embargo Non-Disclosure 
 
What Should You Do? 
If your work has the potential to create impact 
beyond the university, the goal is not to slow 
down your research - it is to make informed 
decisions that preserve your options. Use this 
as a brief path forward: 
1. Submit an Invention Disclosure as 
early as possible: UMKC Invention 
Disclosure Form 
2. Plan your communications. Before 
submitting, presenting, or posting, 
consider the IP consequences. 
3. Ask for guidance. Our office is here to help you navigate the process and protect your 
innovation every step of the way. 
Conclusion 
Sharing your discoveries is an essential part of academic work. But if your goal is for that work to 
create impact beyond the university, how and when you share it matters.
