Helping you move your work beyond the university

whether research can move beyond the university 
and into real-world use. It shapes how effectively

other external stakeholders who ultimately 
determine whether your work advances. Without 
clear communication, even strong ideas can fail to

In this guide, we will explore key aspects of communication and negotiation that are essential for

Where This Fits in the Translation Process 
Moving research beyond the university requires more than a strong idea or technical breakthrough. 
It requires a series of interactions with people who evaluate that work through a different lens, 
including funders, industry partners, investors, and potential users. Communication is the 
mechanism through which those interactions happen. 
 
At each stage of the translation process, researchers are asked to explain what their work does, 
why it matters, and how it could be used. These moments occur in many forms, from grant 
proposals and industry conversations to licensing discussions and startup pitches. In each case, 
the outcome depends not only on the quality of the research, but on how clearly its value can be 
understood by others.

In the context of research translation, communication and negotiation determine whether your 
work can move forward beyond the university and into real-world use. These skills enable 
researchers to secure funding, collaborate effectively, and translate scientific discoveries into 
viable commercial applications.  
 
Strong communication and negotiation abilities empower you to: 
• Secure funding and resources for research and commercialization efforts. 
• Build productive collaborations with peers, industry partners, and stakeholders. 
• Effectively translate scientific discoveries into viable commercial ventures. 
• Navigate business relationships within academia, industry, and entrepreneurship. 
 
These skills show up at critical moments in the translation process. Whether you are writing a grant 
proposal, engaging an industry partner, presenting to a potential funder, or negotiating the terms of 
a collaboration, your ability to communicate clearly and navigate those discussions directly affects 
whether progress is made.

The Role of TLAs (Three-Letter Acronyms) in Business 
One of the defining features of business-focused dialogue is the frequent use of TLAs or Three-
Letter Acronyms, short abbreviations that allow stakeholders to streamline discussions about 
strategy, finance, and project deliverables. Examples include: 
• ROI (Return on Investment), 
• IP (Intellectual Property), 
• KPI (Key Performance Indicator), 
• TAM (Total Addressable Market), and 
• CAC (Customer Acquisition Cost)

Securing Funding and Resources 
Securing funding is one of the most critical aspects of academic and entrepreneurial success. It is 
a competitive process that requires a researcher to articulate their work’s significance, align 
proposals with funder priorities, and successfully negotiate agreements.  
 
In many cases, strong research proposals fall short not because of the underlying work, but 
because the value is not communicated in a way that aligns with how funders or partners make 
decisions. Misalignment between academic framing and external expectations is a common 
source of friction that can slow or prevent progress. 
 
To address these expectations, researchers should underscore how their work can generate 
financial returns, reduce costs, or solve a pressing market need. This approach involves clearly 
describing the broader environment in which the innovation might operate. Market data—such as 
potential customer segments, current competitors, and regulatory landscapes—demonstrates that 
the research isn’t just academically rigorous but also commercially promising. 
 
Communicating your research in a way that resonates with funding agencies, investors, and grant 
committees requires: 
• Persuasive Writing and Speaking: Crafting compelling grant proposals, investment 
pitches, and research presentations that clearly articulate the impact of your work. 
• Audience Awareness: Tailoring your message to meet the interests and priorities of funding 
bodies, ensuring they understand the broader implications of your research. 
• Negotiation Tactics: Successfully negotiating funding terms, budgets, and intellectual 
property agreements to maximize support for your projects. 
 
An effective proposal or presentation highlights core benefits rather than dwelling solely on 
technical depth. The language must speak directly to investors, granting bodies, and corporate 
sponsors by showing them why a particular research project is worth financing.

Building Collaborative Relationships 
Industry collaboration can significantly enhance a research program by bringing real-world 
expertise, market knowledge, and even financial support to academic endeavors. However, 
successful partnerships rely on clear, consistent communication. In many cases, corporate 
partners evaluate a collaboration based on business-driven priorities such as product development 
timelines, competitive advantages, or market readiness. 
 
Effective communication helps you: 
• Align Goals and Expectations: Clearly define roles and responsibilities within research 
teams or business partnerships. 
• Prevent and Resolve Conflicts: Active listening and diplomacy can resolve 
misunderstandings before they escalate. 
• Ensure Recognition and Fair Contributions: Negotiating authorship, contributions, and 
funding allocations fairly enhances long-term partnerships. 
 
When reaching out to potential partners, UMKC researchers are most effective when they provide a 
succinct but comprehensive explanation of what a partnership could achieve. This explanation 
should connect the technical value of the innovation—whether it’s a new manufacturing process 
or a medical diagnostic technology—to the business outcomes that matter most to corporate 
teams. By framing academic work in terms of cost savings, revenue generation, or brand 
enhancement, researchers can make it easier for industry partners to see the mutual benefits of 
collaboration. 
 
Ongoing communication is equally important. Progress reports, virtual meetings, and public-facing 
updates help ensure that all parties stay aligned and that any emerging challenges or opportunities 
are promptly addressed. In this regard, adopting industry best practices—such as creating clear 
milestones and deliverables—fosters trust and encourages future collaboration. 
 
UMKC’s Innovation Center and interdisciplinary research groups foster connections between 
faculty, industry leaders, and entrepreneurs to encourage impactful partnerships. 
Transitioning to Entrepreneurship 
Some UMKC faculty and graduate students go a step further by launching their own ventures to 
commercialize university-based research. In these scenarios, business communication shifts 
from being an asset to being absolutely essential. Founders must navigate pitches for angel 
investors or venture capitalists, recruit early employees or advisors, and build a brand presence—
all of which depend on articulating a compelling story around the innovation.

In entrepreneurship, clarity and conciseness take on added weight. Prospective investors might 
only give a pitch deck a few minutes of attention before deciding whether to pursue further 
discussions. Similarly, potential customers need to quickly grasp the value and utility of a new 
product. Approaching these audiences with concise narratives, well-chosen data points, and a 
savvy use of TLAs can help a scientific breakthrough stand out in a crowded marketplace. 
 
To effectively move an innovation from the lab to the marketplace, you must: 
• Simplify Complex Information: Investors and business partners may not have technical 
expertise; your ability to explain research in clear, compelling language is crucial. 
• Develop a Business Pitch: Crafting and delivering a strong pitch helps attract funding and 
industry partners. 
• Negotiate Business Terms: From equity distribution with co-founders to licensing 
agreements with corporate partners, negotiation skills play a pivotal role in business 
success. 
 
Equally important is the ability to adapt messages to a range of audiences. Investors, for instance, 
may focus on potential return on investment and market size, while customers or end-users are 
more likely to be interested in cost savings, ease of use, and immediate benefits. By tailoring each 
conversation to the priorities of the listener, researchers can significantly improve their startup’s 
prospects for success. 
Key Communication Skills  
When researchers communicate their innovations to potential customers or investors, technical 
expertise alone is not enough. Success hinges on the ability to translate complex ideas into 
compelling narratives, foster engagement through active listening, and navigate discussions with 
emotional intelligence. Below are three essential communication skills that can help researchers 
make a lasting impact. 
Clarity and Conciseness 
Technical innovations often involve intricate details, but potential investors and customers may not 
have the same level of expertise. Researchers must learn to distill complex concepts into clear, 
concise, and accessible language. 
 
• Avoid jargon and technical overload – Use relatable metaphors and real-world 
applications to illustrate how the innovation works and why it matters. 
• Prioritize key messages – Focus on the problem the innovation solves, its benefits, and its 
potential impact, rather than getting lost in technical minutiae. 
• Structure information effectively – Start with a strong value proposition, provide evidence 
of viability, and conclude with a clear call to action.

Active Listening 
Communication is a two-way process. Understanding the concerns, interests, and feedback of 
potential investors or customers can refine messaging and build stronger relationships. 
 
• Listen for key concerns – Investors may focus on market potential and return on 
investment, while customers may prioritize ease of use and affordability. 
• Ask clarifying questions – Engage in meaningful dialogue by seeking to understand their 
needs, rather than simply presenting information. 
• Adapt based on feedback – Demonstrating responsiveness to concerns fosters trust and 
credibility. 
Emotional Intelligence 
Emotional intelligence (EQ) is critical in high-stakes conversations, helping researchers navigate 
negotiations, manage differing perspectives, and establish rapport. 
 
• Read nonverbal cues – Pay attention to body language and tone of voice to gauge interest 
or hesitation. 
• Manage emotions effectively – Stay composed under pressure and respond thoughtfully 
rather than reactively. 
• Empathize with your audience – Understanding the motivations and concerns of investors 
and customers can help tailor messaging in a way that resonates. 
 
Researchers must remember that great communication is not just about conveying information but 
about making meaningful connections. By mastering clarity, active listening, and emotional 
intelligence, they can position their innovations for success, foster trust with stakeholders, and 
ultimately drive commercialization forward. 
Essential Negotiation Skills 
For researchers seeking to commercialize their innovations, negotiation skills are just as important 
as technical expertise. Whether discussing funding with investors, licensing agreements with 
companies, or partnerships with industry leaders, effective negotiation can shape the success of 
an innovation’s journey to market. Below are three essential negotiation skills researchers should 
develop. 
Preparation and Planning 
Successful negotiations start well before the conversation begins. Understanding your innovation’s 
value, potential market, and the priorities of the other party sets the stage for productive 
discussions. 
• Know your numbers – Be prepared with data on market size, customer demand, 
development costs, and projected revenue. Investors and partners will expect a solid 
business case.

• Define your goals and limits – Determine your ideal outcomes, acceptable compromises, 
and absolute deal-breakers in advance. 
• Research the other party – Understanding the motivations, priorities, and constraints of 
investors or potential partners allows for more strategic discussions. 
 
Persuasive Communication 
How you present your case can significantly influence the outcome of a negotiation. The ability to 
articulate value and address concerns persuasively is crucial. 
 
• Lead with benefits, not just features – Investors care about financial returns, while 
customers and partners look for real-world benefits. Position your innovation in terms of its 
impact. 
• Use storytelling – A compelling narrative about the problem your innovation solves and its 
potential success can make your pitch more memorable. 
• Demonstrate flexibility – Being open to alternative deal structures, such as phased 
investments or milestone-based agreements, can keep negotiations moving forward. 
 
Navigating the Conversation 
Negotiations can be high-stakes and sometimes tense. Emotional intelligence helps researchers 
stay in control, build rapport, and create win-win outcomes. 
• Read the room – Paying attention to verbal and nonverbal cues can reveal when to push 
forward and when to step back. 
• Stay calm under pressure – Investors or partners may test your resolve. Maintaining 
composure and responding thoughtfully builds credibility. 
• Build relationships, not just transactions – A collaborative, problem-solving approach 
often leads to better long-term partnerships than a purely competitive stance. 
 
Negotiation is not about winning or losing—it’s about creating mutually beneficial agreements that 
bring innovations to market successfully. By preparing strategically, communicating persuasively, 
and leveraging emotional intelligence, researchers can navigate negotiations effectively and secure 
the resources needed to bring their innovations to life.
