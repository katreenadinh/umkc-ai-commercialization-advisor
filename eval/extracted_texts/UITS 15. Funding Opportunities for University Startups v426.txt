Funding to Move Research Toward Real-World Impact 
Helping you move your work beyond the university  
 
Research with real-world potential often needs 
resources to move forward. Whether that next step 
involves technical validation, customer discovery, 
prototype development, regulatory planning, 
partnership building, or startup formation, progress 
usually depends on securing the right support at the 
right time. 
 
In the UMKC Impact & Translation Series, funding 
should be understood as a tool for reducing 
uncertainty and enabling progress. Funding is not 
the goal by itself. It is one of the mechanisms that 
can help move research, innovation, and creative 
work beyond the university and closer to real-world 
use. 
 
For some projects, that support may come through 
grants, proof-of-concept funds, or university-backed 
programs. For others, it may involve angel 
investment, venture capital, strategic partnerships, or revenue-based approaches. The right source 
depends on the nature of the work, the pathway to impact, and the milestone that needs to be 
reached next. 
 
This whitepaper outlines the major categories of funding that may support translation, 
commercialization, and startup development at UMKC. It is designed to help faculty and graduate 
students think more clearly about which funding sources fit which stage of progress, what those 
sources are intended to support, and when it makes sense to engage the Office of 
Commercialization in exploring next steps. 
 
Grant-Based Funding 
Grant funding is often the most appropriate early source of support for university-connected 
innovations because it can help reduce risk without requiring founders to give up ownership. In 
many cases, grants support the work that must happen before a startup, licensing effort, or external 
partnership is viable. That may include feasibility studies, prototype development, customer 
discovery, validation work, or early commercialization planning.

Small Business Innovation Research (SBIR) and Small Business 
Technology Transfer (STTR) Grants 
The SBIR and STTR programs offer federal funding to small businesses conducting research and 
development with the potential for commercialization. These grants are highly competitive but 
provide significant financial support: 
• Phase I Grants: Typically up to $150,000, supporting feasibility studies. 
• Phase II Grants: Can reach up to $1 million, funding further development. 
• Phase III: While no direct funding is provided, businesses that reach this stage often secure 
contracts with government agencies. 
 
SBIR/STTR grants provide substantial funding without requiring dilution of equity, allowing 
university-affiliated entrepreneurs to explore market applications of their research.  
 
Additionally, STTR grants require collaboration with a nonprofit research institution, making them 
ideal for university researchers who want to work with industry partners while maintaining strong 
ties to their academic work. 
 
Successful SBIR/STTR applications demonstrate: 
• A clear path to commercialization 
• Strong market validation 
• Feasible technical milestones and a well-structured team 
• Potential customer engagement 
• Letters of support from industry partners or potential customers 
 
Many faculty researchers seek SBIR/STTR grants to fund technology validation, prototype 
development, and initial market testing, which are necessary steps before securing private 
investment or forming a startup. 
 
Government Granting Agencies and the Current Funding Environment 
Federal agencies such as the National Institutes of Health (NIH), National Science Foundation 
(NSF), Department of Energy (DOE), and Department of Defense (DOD) provide substantial grant 
funding for early-stage research and commercialization. These agencies support university-
affiliated startups, offering both early-stage development grants and later-stage commercialization 
grants. 
 
However, the landscape of government grant funding is currently in flux. With ongoing shifts in 
administration policies, research priorities, and budget allocations, funding availability and focus 
areas may change. Startups should closely monitor federal funding announcements, participate in 
grant workshops, and leverage university resources to stay ahead of policy changes that might 
impact their eligibility for funding.

Over the last few years, federal funding priorities have shifted to focus on strategic areas that 
present strong commercialization opportunities. Some notable trends include: 
• Artificial Intelligence (AI) and Machine Learning: Many federal agencies, including NSF 
and NIH, are allocating significant funding for AI applications in healthcare, security, and 
manufacturing. 
• Climate Tech and Sustainability: DOE and NSF have increased funding for energy storage, 
renewable energy solutions, and decarbonization technologies. 
• Biotechnology and Precision Medicine: NIH and DoD are investing in personalized 
medicine, genomic research, and bioinformatics to advance targeted healthcare solutions. 
• Supply Chain Resilience: Federal funding programs now emphasize advanced 
manufacturing and logistics solutions to address global supply chain challenges. 
 
UMKC researchers should consider how their innovations align with these funding priorities when 
developing grant proposals and commercialization strategies. Engaging with the Missouri SBDC can 
help navigate these uncertainties and identify the most viable opportunities. 
 
State, University, and Private Foundation Grants 
Many private foundations and university-backed programs offer funding for early-stage research 
and commercialization efforts. These grants can provide critical early-stage support, particularly for 
technologies in the proof-of-concept phase. 
 
Missouri Technology Corporation (MTC) provides state-level support for innovative startups through 
matching funds that complement federal and private investments. 
• How It Works: MTC offers funding to startups that have secured other sources of 
investment, such as SBIR/STTR grants or venture capital. These matching funds help reduce 
the financial burden on early-stage companies and enable them to accelerate 
commercialization efforts. 
• Why It Matters for UMKC Startups: University-affiliated startups can leverage MTC 
matching funds to expand their research, hire personnel, and advance product 
development. This additional funding can increase a company’s ability to attract investors 
by demonstrating strong support from both federal and state sources. 
 
Proof-of-Concept Funds  
Proof-of-concept (POC) funds are critical resources that help early-stage startups validate their 
ideas and develop working prototypes before seeking larger investments. These funds provide 
financial support for activities such as prototype development, market validation, customer 
discovery, and early-stage business formation. 
 
One such example is Digital Sandbox KC, a program that offers proof-of-concept funding of up to 
$20,000 to early-stage startups in the Kansas City region. The goal of Digital Sandbox is to reduce 
the financial risk associated with early-stage innovation by providing grants for critical activities that 
help move an idea closer to commercialization. Unlike traditional investment sources, Digital

Stages of Equity-Based Funding 
Equity-based funding involves securing investment from external parties in exchange for partial 
ownership of the startup. This type of funding is crucial for scaling a business but requires careful 
negotiation to retain strategic control. 
 
Startups that seek equity-based funding typically progress through several funding stages, each 
with unique opportunities and challenges.

Below is an overview of the major funding stages and the available sources of capital for each. 
 
Pre-Seed Funding 
Pre-seed funding covers early expenses like market research and developing a minimal viable 
product (MVP). Most pre-seed funding comes from founders’ savings or personal networks.

• Bootstrapping: Using personal savings or revenue generated by the business to fund 
operations is common at this stage. Founders maintain full control, but limited resources 
can slow growth. 
• Friends and Family: This form of funding is typically the easiest to secure but requires 
careful consideration of legal agreements to avoid misunderstandings. Friends and family 
investors may not expect significant returns but can provide a quick cash infusion. 
 
Seed Funding 
Seed funding helps startups cover early-stage development costs such as product design and team 
hiring. 
• Angel Investors: Wealthy individuals who invest in exchange for equity. In addition to 
funding, angel investors often offer valuable mentorship and industry connections. 
• Incubators and Accelerators: Startup accelerators provide funding, mentorship, and 
networking opportunities to help startups refine their products and scale operations. 
Incubators add the element of “collision” space, helping startup entrepreneurs benefit from 
proximity to other growing companies.  
 
Early-Stage Funding 
Early-stage funding is critical for scaling operations and moving from a prototype to a fully 
commercialized product. 
• Venture Capital (VC): Venture capital firms invest in high-growth startups with proven 
business models. In exchange for equity, VCs provide large sums of money and often take 
an active role in shaping the company’s direction. Venture capitalists are looking for 
scalability, a large market opportunity, and a clear exit strategy. 
• Incubators and Accelerators: These programs not only provide funding but also 
mentorship, office space, and networking opportunities to help startups scale quickly. 
Accelerators often take equity in exchange for services and may culminate in a demo day 
where startups pitch to investors. 
 
Growth Stage Funding 
Once a startup achieves product-market fit and is ready to scale further, growth-stage funding 
provides the capital necessary to expand. 
• Series A, B, and C Funding: As a startup grows, it may go through multiple rounds of 
venture funding, with each round increasing in size. Series A focuses on scaling operations, 
while Series B and C target geographic expansion and product diversification. For instance, 
Series A funding typically ranges from $2 million to $15 million. Later rounds, such as Series 
B or C, can involve tens of millions of dollars. 
 
Late-Stage Funding 
For mature startups, late-stage funding helps them expand globally, acquire other companies, or 
prepare for an IPO.

• Private Equity: Private equity funds invest in more established startups, often preparing 
them for acquisitions or public offerings. These funds typically seek higher returns and a 
clear exit strategy, either through an acquisition or IPO. 
• Initial Public Offering (IPO): When a company goes public, it offers shares to the general 
market. This is a common exit strategy for startups at the peak of their growth but carries 
significant risks, including market volatility. 
 
Alternative Funding Sources 
For startups that may not qualify for venture capital or prefer to explore alternative funding options, 
there are several other sources of capital. 
• Crowdfunding: Platforms like Kickstarter and Indiegogo allow startups to raise capital by 
pitching their ideas to the public. This method is particularly useful for consumer-facing 
products and helps test market demand before full-scale production. Equity crowdfunding 
platforms such as SeedInvest or Crowdcube allow startups to raise funds in exchange for 
shares. The most successful crowdfunded businesses have a few things in common: 
o Don’t start without 75%+ committed – Crowdfunding campaigns rely on the 
excitement generated around an innovation, and products that show significant 
jumps in the first 48 hours of the campaign rise to the top of the promotion list.  
o Set a realistic goal, blow it away – Realize that with most platforms, you won’t 
receive the funds unless you have met your fundraising goal. So set a realistic goal 
and hit it as quickly as possible with pre-loaded interest. If there’s enough interest, it 
may grow to a multiple of your original goal. Know what you need to close versus 
what you’d like to close.  
o Promote Aggressively: Use social media, press releases, and influencer 
partnerships to extend your campaign’s reach. Regular updates keep backers 
engaged and build trust, increasing the likelihood of additional support and future 
funding opportunities. Encourage additional funding by offering new incentives 
when initial goals are met. 
 
• Loans: Startups can apply for small business loans through the Small Business 
Administration (SBA). SBA loans provide low-interest rates and favorable terms for 
qualifying startups. Other loan options include credit card loans or short-term loans to 
cover immediate cash flow needs. The challenge is that banks only loan money if you can 
demonstrate the ability to pay the loan back; for many pre-profit startups, bank loans are 
not a viable avenue of funding.  
 
• Pitch Competitions: Pitch competitions provide an excellent opportunity for startups to 
secure funding while gaining visibility and feedback from investors. UMKC and other 
organizations host competitions where entrepreneurs pitch their ideas to panels of judges 
for a chance to win funding and mentorship. Programs such as the Regnier Venture Creation 
Challenge and national competitions like Rice Business Plan Competition offer cash prizes, 
networking opportunities, and investor connections to promising startups.

Strategies for Securing Funding 
Successfully securing funding involves more than just identifying the right sources. University 
startups must develop strong strategies to make their businesses attractive to investors. 
• Build a Strong Team: Investors look for well-rounded teams with both technical and 
business expertise. Founders should focus on building teams with a strong "founder-market 
fit," meaning team members are deeply familiar with the problems they aim to solve. 
Investors look for well-balanced teams with a mix of technical expertise, business acumen, 
and execution capability. The ideal startup team includes: 
o Technical Lead (University Researcher/Inventor): Provides deep expertise in the 
technology, innovation, and research aspects.

o Business Lead (CEO or Entrepreneurial Co-Founder): Manages business strategy,

o Product/Operations Lead: Ensures the product is developed efficiently and 
delivered to market. 
o Sales & Marketing Lead: Drives customer acquisition and revenue growth. 
 
Faculty members and graduate students often serve as the technical lead, developing and 
refining the innovation. However, they may need to partner with experienced business 
professionals to manage commercialization and investor relations effectively. 
 
• Develop a Clear Exit Strategy: Investors, particularly venture capitalists and angel 
investors, want to know how and when they will see a return on their investment. A clear 
path to acquisition, IPO, or licensing deal can be a strong selling point. 
 
• Leverage Grants and University Resources: Securing non-dilutive funding such as SBIR or 
STTR grants is highly attractive to venture capitalists, as it means the startup requires less 
external funding and retains more equity. UMKC’s support in grant writing and the 
availability of internal university funding sources can be invaluable in this respect. 
 
 
Conclusion 
Funding can play an important role in helping research move toward real-world use, but the most 
useful funding is tied to a specific purpose. In some cases, that purpose is technical validation. In 
others, it is market discovery, prototype development, team building, partnership readiness, or 
startup formation. The goal is not simply to secure money. The goal is to reduce uncertainty, reach 
the next meaningful milestone, and strengthen the pathway through which your work could create 
impact. 
 
At UMKC, faculty and graduate students do not need to have a company, a polished pitch, or a 
complete plan in order to begin that conversation. If your work may have potential beyond the 
university, the Office of Commercialization can help you think through what kind of support makes 
sense, what stage you are in, and what next step is most worth pursuing.
