Why Commercialization Matters for Research Impact  
Helping you move your work beyond the university  
 
In today’s research environment, success is no longer 
measured by publication alone. UMKC researchers 
possess the creativity and expertise to generate world-
changing ideas. But impact does not stop at discovery.  
 
Many researchers are ultimately motivated by impact - 
seeing their work improve lives, influence practice, or 
solve real problems beyond the university. 
 
In many cases, that kind of impact requires translation. 
One pathway for doing that is commercialization: the 
process of moving research into real-world use through 
structured development, partnerships, or deployment. 
 
This whitepaper explores the why, what, and how of 
commercialization at UMKC, emphasizing the 
opportunity and responsibility researchers have to 
bring innovation to the public through engagement with the Office of Commercialization. 
Introduction: Research That Reaches Beyond the Lab 
Universities have long been engines of knowledge creation. But in today’s interconnected and 
impact-driven research landscape, discovery alone is no longer sufficient. Public and private 
funders are now emphasizing societal benefit and seeking clear pathways from lab to life. 
 
Commercialization, when done ethically and strategically, helps bridge this gap. It provides a 
structured route for bringing innovations to the public while supporting the broader mission of 
higher education: to advance knowledge and improve lives. 
 
At UMKC, researchers in medicine, engineering, music therapy, the social sciences, and beyond are 
already embracing this pathway. They are not just publishing papers—they’re creating startups, 
licensing technologies, and collaborating with industry to deliver meaningful outcomes. 
How Translation Increases the Impact of Your Work 
These benefits reflect what happens when research successfully moves into real-world use, 
whether through commercialization, partnerships, or other applied pathways.

1. Accelerated Societal Impact 
Traditional dissemination through journal articles reaches other scholars, but it often falls short of 
creating real-world change. Commercialization brings discoveries out of the lab and into the hands 
of the people who need them. 
 
Examples of impact: 
• Medical devices that deliver better care to underserved patients 
• AI algorithms applied to climate risk or public health 
• Tools and therapies made accessible through commercial distribution 
 
As emphasized in EMBO Reports, the societal impact of research can be difficult to measure, but it 
is increasingly demanded by policymakers and funders. Even basic science, once seen as 
untouchable in its purity, is being reevaluated through the lens of public benefit. 
 
2. Scientific Responsibility and Societal Ethics 
Commercialization isn’t just a strategic move - it’s a moral imperative. As Stephanie J. Bird argues in 
Socially Responsible Science Is More than “Good Science”, scientists hold a collective 
responsibility to consider the foreseeable impacts of their research. Society expects more than 
technical rigor; it expects relevance, equity, and foresight. 
 
Commercialization helps fulfill this broader ethical commitment by: 
• Bringing public investments in science full circle through societal returns 
• Ensuring that innovations do not stay siloed in academia 
• Encouraging scientists to engage in ethical foresight and policy dialogue 
 
3. Enhanced Research Visibility and Value 
Engaging in commercialization draws new attention to your work. Researchers who develop 
translational pathways often see: 
• Invitations to participate in applied research centers 
• Features in Whiteboard2Boardroom videos and innovation showcases 
• Competitive advantages in funding proposals, including SBIR/STTR grants and translational 
research calls 
 
UMKC’s Office of Commercialization ensures that your innovations are visible, evaluated, and 
strategically promoted. 
 
4. Economic Growth and Entrepreneurial Opportunity 
By commercializing, faculty contribute directly to Kansas City’s economic ecosystem. Pathways 
include: 
• Launching startups that create local jobs 
• Earning licensing revenue that supports labs and graduate students 
• Accessing funding from entrepreneurial programs like Digital Sandbox KC 
 
Your innovation doesn’t just serve society - it fuels it.

5. Stronger Industry Partnerships 
Commercial-ready research attracts interest from companies, non-profits, and government 
agencies, leading to: 
• Joint research agreements 
• Industry-led beta testing 
• Early adoption in applied settings 
 
UMKC’s growing partnerships in fields like health tech, engineering, and education are often

Understanding the Hesitations: What Holds Researchers Back? 
Despite these benefits, national studies and faculty interviews consistently show a few recurring 
challenges: 
 
“I’m not a businessperson.” - You don’t have to 
be. The Office of Commercialization provides 
business modeling, IP support, and startup 
guidance. Many researchers stay focused on the 
science while partnering with entrepreneurs and 
staff experts. 
 
“I don’t have time.” - You already invest years 
developing your discoveries. A 30-minute 
conversation with our team can clarify whether 
a path to market exists. The earlier you engage, 
the smoother the process. 
 
“Isn’t commercialization a distraction from 
academic rigor?” - No. In fact, 
commercialization can strengthen your academic output. Studies show that faculty who patent or 
license technologies often have higher publication productivity than their peers.

Case Study: Advancing Disease Detection Through Licensing 
Innovation: Lectin-based detection of glycoRNA 
Researcher: Dr. Mingui Fu, UMKC School of Medicine 
Field: Biomedical Diagnostics 
 
Dr. Mingui Fu developed a novel approach to detecting glycoRNA using lectin-based methods—
offering the potential for dramatically simpler and more accessible testing for cancer, immune 
disorders, and infectious diseases. Recognizing the translational potential of this breakthrough, Dr.

Case Study: From Lullabies to Lung Health 
Innovation: In-bassinet music-reward device for preterm infants 
Researcher: Dr. Amy Robertson, UMKC Conservatory (in collaboration with Children’s Mercy 
Hospital) 
Field: Music Therapy, Neonatal Medicine 
 
Dr. Amy Robertson’s research on music therapy revealed that premature infants perceive tone and 
melody as a form of positive reward. Motivated by the potential clinical implications, she asked a 
powerful question: could music be used as a reinforcement tool to encourage spontaneous 
breathing in NICU infants? 
 
Working alongside physicians at Children’s Mercy Hospital’s NICU, Dr. Robertson developed a 
hypothesis: playing a short music clip immediately after a spontaneous breath might prompt more 
voluntary breathing, reducing infants’ time on mechanical ventilation and the associated medical 
risks. 
 
However, the concept presented a logistical challenge—deploying live musicians in the NICU was 
not feasible. To scale the idea, she envisioned an automated in-bassinet device capable of 
detecting spontaneous breaths and delivering an audio reward. 
 
Dr. Robertson brought this concept to the UMKC Office of Commercialization, where we helped her 
refine and define the scope of her innovation. Our team provided: 
• Design and strategy support to translate her concept into an actionable development plan 
• Connections to industry advisors to consult on device feasibility and sensor integration

Getting Started: Action Steps for UMKC Researchers 
If you’re working through the UMKC Impact & Translation Series, these are the natural next steps to 
begin applying what you’ve learned: 
1. Talk with Us Early - Before you publish or present findings that could be commercially 
relevant, contact the Office of Commercialization. We’ll help you understand your options. 
2. Submit an Invention Disclosure - This form kicks off internal evaluation and starts the 
protection process. 
3. Explore Opportunities - Join community speaker series or commercialization workshops to 
learn more about available resources. 
4. Consider All Pathways - Not every innovation becomes a startup. Licensing, nonprofit 
deployment, open science models, and hybrid approaches are all supported. 
5. Build a Team - Work with the Office and partners like Whiteboard2Boardroom, Digital 
Sandbox, or entrepreneurial students to strengthen your project’s success potential. 
Conclusion: Your Ideas Deserve to Go Further 
This is not about changing the nature of your work. It is about expanding its reach. 
 
Your research already has value. The question is how that value might move beyond the university 
and what it would take to get it there. 
 
The UMKC Impact & Translation Series is designed to help you think more clearly about that path. 
The Office of Commercialization is here to help you explore it.
