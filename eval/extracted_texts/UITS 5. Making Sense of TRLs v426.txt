Making Sense of Technology Readiness Levels 
A Practical Framework for Moving Your Work Toward Real-World Impact  
 
Technology Readiness Levels (TRLs) provide a 
structured, universal framework to assess the 
maturity of innovations as they move from initial 
discovery to market adoption. Originally developed 
by NASA, TRLs are now used across federal 
agencies and industries to evaluate readiness and 
mitigate risks.  
 
For UMKC researchers, understanding TRLs offers a 
strategic advantage in project planning, 
commercialization, and industry engagement. This 
whitepaper introduces the TRL framework and 
explores how it applies across diverse academic 
disciplines, from medicine to the arts. 
 
This guide is not intended to make you an expert in 
Technology Readiness Levels. It is intended to help 
you use TRLs as a practical tool to better understand 
where your work stands today, what risks remain, and what would need to happen for that work to 
function outside the university. 
Why TRLs Matter at UMKC 
At UMKC, faculty members contribute transformative ideas across a wide array of disciplines. 
Whether developing life-saving therapies, advancing next-generation technologies, or creating 
groundbreaking artistic works, researchers often face the same question: "How ready is this 
innovation for real-world application?"  
 
Technology Readiness Levels offer a clear answer. These nine levels, which mark the journey from 
concept to full deployment, allow innovators to communicate maturity levels with funders, 
partners, and technology transfer offices.  
 
Importantly, TRLs also help identify necessary steps to advance a project—whether that means 
prototyping, clinical testing, or regulatory navigation. Understanding this framework positions 
UMKC researchers to more effectively translate discoveries into societal impact.

Within the UMKC Impact & Translation Series, TRLs serve as one way to think about readiness. They 
do not measure how good or innovative an idea is. They help clarify how much uncertainty has been 
reduced and how close that work is to functioning in a real-world setting. 
Understanding the TRL Scale 
TRLs offer a shared vocabulary to describe the evolution of an idea into a usable, validated solution. 
Each level marks a critical milestone that builds upon the last, creating a roadmap from research to 
deployment.  
 
 
TRL 1: Foundational Discovery - Basic principles have been observed. This level includes 
early-stage research and published findings that form the basis for a new idea. 
 
TRL 2: Concept Formulation - Practical applications are identified, but remain speculative. 
Initial hypotheses and theoretical models begin to take shape. 
 
TRL 3: Experimental Proof of Concept - Laboratory studies validate core assumptions. 
Experiments demonstrate that the concept works under controlled conditions. 
 
TRL 4: Lab-Based Validation - Key components are built and tested in a laboratory setting. 
The system or process shows functionality and feasibility in a simplified environment. 
 
TRL 5: Simulated Environment Testing - Prototypes or components are validated in 
environments that mimic real-world conditions. This marks the beginning of bridging lab 
and field.

TRL 6: Relevant Environment Demonstration - A system or prototype is tested in a setting 
that reflects real operating conditions. This is typically the final step before operational field 
testing. 
 
TRL 7: Operational Environment Demonstration - The prototype is deployed in an actual 
operating environment. The system is integrated with real-world infrastructure or workflows. 
 
TRL 8: Final System Qualification - The complete system undergoes rigorous testing and 
quality assurance to meet all operational criteria. Final refinements are made. 
 
TRL 9: Proven System in Use - The technology is in full use and has proven effective 
through successful mission operations or market validation. 
 
Most academic research begins in TRL 1–3. Real-world use typically requires reaching TRL 6–9. 
Each of these levels provides a critical opportunity for UMKC researchers to assess progress, plan 
next steps, and communicate readiness to stakeholders. 
How TRLs Apply Across UMKC Research Fields 
TRLs are not just for aerospace and engineering. Their structured approach to development applies 
across UMKC's diverse research landscape.  
 
TRLs focus on technical development, but technical readiness alone is not enough to create 
impact. Work may function in a lab or controlled setting and still fail to translate if it does not align 
with real-world conditions, users, or constraints. 
 
As you assess your work, it is important to consider both: 
• Technical readiness (Does it work?)  
• Real-world readiness (Does it matter? Will it be used?)  
 
Strong translation requires progress on both dimensions. Understanding how TRLs map to your 
field helps clarify goals, timelines, and partnership opportunities. 
 
Drug Development: From Molecules to Medicine 
Translating laboratory discoveries into safe, effective therapies is a long and complex process. Drug 
development involves rigorous evaluation to ensure both safety and efficacy before a compound 
ever reaches a patient.  
 
The TRL framework maps this journey clearly—from understanding molecular mechanisms to 
navigating human trials and regulatory approvals—helping researchers anticipate the milestones 
and requirements needed for each stage.

Basic Research (TRL 1-3) - Exploration of disease mechanisms and initial compound 
screening define early progress in pharmaceuticals. This work establishes theoretical and 
empirical groundwork. 
 
Preclinical Testing (TRL 4-5) – Candidate drugs undergo lab-based and animal studies to 
assess safety and efficacy. Validation here supports decisions to move into human trials. 
 
Clinical Trials (TRL 6-7) - Phase I and II trials test the drug in humans, assessing dosage, 
effectiveness, and side effects. Success indicates real-world feasibility. 
 
Market Readiness (TRL 8-9) - Phase III trials and FDA approval represent system 
qualification. Once the drug is prescribed and in use, it reaches full TRL 9 maturity. 
 
Medical Devices: Engineering with Impact 
Medical devices are at the intersection of engineering, health sciences, and regulatory oversight. 
Whether developing wearable sensors, imaging systems, or surgical tools, innovators must 
demonstrate both technical performance and patient safety.  
 
TRLs provide a structured lens through which UMKC researchers can advance from a promising 
concept to a clinically accepted tool, highlighting the steps from benchtop validation to human 
trials and full deployment in care settings. 
 
Concept and Feasibility (TRL 1-3) - Innovators explore materials, mechanisms, and user 
needs. Early-stage prototypes and simulations guide the design. 
 
Bench Testing (TRL 4-6) - Devices are tested in labs and simulated environments. FDA 
design controls often begin during this phase to prepare for regulatory pathways. 
 
Human Trials and Approvals (TRL 7-9) - Clinical trials, 510(k) clearance, or PMA approval 
bring the technology into clinical practice, validating safety and effectiveness. 
 
Engineering Projects: Building Toward Real-World Solutions 
Engineering innovation often begins with theoretical models and progresses through iterative 
design, testing, and deployment. Whether focused on infrastructure, energy systems, or digital 
platforms, projects must evolve from feasibility to function in real-world environments. TRLs help 
engineering researchers at UMKC chart a logical path from initial sketches to robust, field-tested 
solutions—ensuring each stage of development meets increasing levels of rigor and relevance. 
 
Design and Modeling (TRL 1-3) - Engineers use sketches, CAD models, and theoretical 
analyses to explore a concept's feasibility and potential.

Prototype Development (TRL 4-6) - Physical builds and lab-based performance testing are 
conducted. Teams begin integrating components into working systems. 
 
Deployment and Optimization (TRL 7-9) - Solutions are tested in the field—on roadways, 
buildings, or data networks. Refinements occur through real-world performance feedback. 
 
Creative and Digital Works: Innovation in Expression 
Creative works and digital experiences follow their own arc of development, from inspiration and 
experimentation to release and audience engagement.  
 
For artists, designers, and digital developers, TRLs offer a way to frame the creative process within a 
development pathway—recognizing the maturation of ideas through prototyping, refinement, and 
real-world interaction. This perspective empowers UMKC creators to plan, iterate, and launch with 
greater strategic clarity. 
 
Ideation and Mockups (TRL 1-3) - Artists, designers, and developers explore themes and 
create initial drafts, whether musical compositions, visual concepts, or code frameworks. 
 
Prototype and Beta Testing (TRL 4-6) - Works take form in testable formats such as demo 
recordings, app betas, or interactive installations. 
 
Public Release (TRL 7-9) - Distribution, exhibition, or public use validates impact and 
readiness. When a digital tool or artistic work is actively engaged with by its intended 
audience, it reaches maturity. 
 
This cross-sector versatility makes TRLs an invaluable planning and communication tool for 
UMKC's research community. 
Integrating TRLs with Manufacturing Readiness 
Once technologies reach the mid-to-late TRLs, questions of scale, consistency, and supply chain 
reliability come into focus. That’s where Manufacturing Readiness Levels (MRLs) align with TRLs to 
guide decisions around production. 
 
Prototype Manufacturing (MRL 4-6) - Processes are developed to produce small batches 
of the innovation. Key challenges such as quality assurance and repeatability are 
addressed. 
 
Full Production Capability (MRL 7-9) - Systems are in place for large-scale manufacturing, 
including process controls, workforce training, and supplier management. This stage aligns 
closely with TRL 8-9, ensuring the technology is not only usable but also reproducible at 
scale.

In practice, TRLs and MRLs often advance together as work moves closer to real-world use. Ignoring 
manufacturing readiness too long can delay or prevent impact. UMKC researchers working on 
products with commercial potential should begin MRL planning by TRL 5-6 to align timelines and 
resources effectively. 
Planning Next Steps Using 
TRLs at UMKC 
TRLs are most valuable when used to guide 
decision-making and project development. At 
UMKC, researchers can leverage the framework 
to clarify needs and unlock resources. 
 
1. Self-Assess Your Project - Identify your 
current TRL and what evidence supports 
that assessment. Then define what 
would need to be true to move to the 
next level. 
 
2. Meet with the Office of Commercialization - Use TRLs as a starting point for conversation, 
not a conclusion. UMKC’s commercialization staff can help verify your TRL stage, identify 
funding sources, and connect you with development partners or mentors. 
 
3. Incorporate TRLs into Grant Proposals - Federal agencies increasingly require a TRL 
assessment in SBIR, STTR, and translational grant applications. Including this framework 
signals preparedness. 
 
4. Leverage Peer Learning Opportunities - Join faculty learning communities or innovation 
lunches to workshop TRL strategies and see how others at UMKC have navigated their 
journey. 
 
The goal is not to assign a number to your work. The goal is to clarify what needs to happen next. By 
consistently applying this model, researchers can more confidently navigate the path to impact. 
Conclusion: A Shared Framework for Innovation Success 
Technology Readiness Levels provide UMKC researchers with a structured lens through which to 
view their innovation journey. From initial insight to public benefit, TRLs clarify both where you are 
and what must come next.  
 
Importantly, this model transcends disciplines and makes it easier for faculty to align with industry, 
investors, and funders.
