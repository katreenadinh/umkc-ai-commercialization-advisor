Protecting Your Innovation: A Guide to Intellectual Property at UMKC 
A Whitepaper for UMKC Faculty Researchers and Graduate Students  
 
When you make a breakthrough in the lab or develop 
a novel solution to a problem, one important 
question follows: could this work create impact 
beyond the university? 
 
In many cases, the answer depends on whether that 
work can be protected, shared appropriately, and 
developed with external partners. Intellectual 
property (IP) is not just a legal step. It is part of how 
research moves from discovery into real-world use. 
 
At UMKC, we want researchers and innovators to 
feel empowered and supported as they navigate the 
path from idea to impact. That path starts with 
understanding the different types of IP, and how 
each plays a role in protecting your discovery. 
 
This does not mean every project needs a patent or a 
startup. It means that understanding protection 
early helps preserve your options for how your work could create impact. 
 
What Is Intellectual Property (IP), and Why Should You Care? 
Intellectual property refers to creations of the mind: inventions, methods, artistic works, designs, 
even brand names. Unlike physical property, IP is intangible, but just as valuable. If you’ve ever 
written software, engineered a device, designed a study protocol, or created an algorithm, you’ve 
likely created IP. 
 
For university researchers, IP does more than secure credit. It enables partnerships, supports 
investment in development, and increases the likelihood that your work can be used outside the 
university. Knowing which form of protection applies to your work is the first step.

Patents 
Patents apply to inventions - things like novel devices, chemical compounds, or processes. A 
patent gives you (and the university, as co-owner) the exclusive right to make, use, or license the 
invention for 20 years from the date of filing. To be eligible, the invention must be new, useful, and 
non-obvious, three key criteria the U.S. Patent and Trademark Office (USPTO) will assess. 
 
There are two main types: provisional patents, which secure a filing date with a simpler application, 
and non-provisional patents, which are the full applications reviewed by the USPTO. Provisional 
patents are valid for one year and give you time to test your innovation or attract partners before 
committing to the full filing. 
 
Copyrights 
If your work involves writing, code, graphics, training materials, or digital content, copyright may 
apply. Copyrights protect the expression of ideas, not the idea itself, and are automatically granted 
when a work is created in tangible form. However, registering the copyright provides added legal 
strength, especially if you need to defend your rights in court. 
 
Trademarks 
Trademarks protect the words, logos, or symbols you use to identify a product or service. While not 
always relevant early in academic research, trademarks become important when you're branding a 
product, spinout company, or platform. A registered trademark provides national protection and 
makes it easier to enforce your brand identity. 
 
Trade Secrets 
Some innovations can’t or shouldn’t be patented. Maybe they’re not patentable, or maybe keeping 
them confidential offers more competitive advantage. In those cases, trade secrets (confidential 
information like formulas, processes, or methods) can be protected by keeping them secure 
through contracts and internal controls. At UMKC, if a research tool or method falls into this 
category, our Office of Technology Transfer can advise on the best way to keep it protected while 
still enabling collaboration. 
 
Making Smart IP Decisions Early 
One of the most important decisions you will make is whether to protect your work before sharing it 
publicly. Here's why that matters. 
 
When you publicly disclose your work through a journal article, poster, or conference presentation, 
you may inadvertently lose your ability to secure a patent. In the U.S., you have one year after 
disclosure to file, but in many other countries, publication removes the possibility of patent 
protection entirely. This is one of the most common ways researchers unintentionally lose future 
options for their work, and why we encourage researchers to contact the Office of Technology 
Transfer before public dissemination.

A key part of early-stage protection is conducting a prior art search - a process where we look for 
existing patents, publications, or products that may overlap with your innovation. This helps 
determine novelty and strengthen your application. It also helps ensure we’re investing university 
resources into innovations with clear commercial potential. 
 
Working with UMKC's Offices: Who Does What? 
Navigating IP doesn’t have to be overwhelming. UMKC offers support through two key offices, and 
each plays a distinct role: 
• The Office of Technology Transfer (OTT): Focuses on IP management such as filing 
patents, reviewing invention disclosures, and tracking legal requirements. If you’re filing an 
invention disclosure or need to understand your rights as a university researcher, this is your 
starting point. 
• The Office of Commercialization (OC): Supports the broader innovation journey like 
helping you identify partners, assess the market potential of your invention, explore startup 
opportunities, and prepare materials to promote your innovation to potential licensees. 
 
Engaging early with both teams sets your project up for success. We can help you explore whether 
your invention is a good candidate for a patent, whether a startup might be viable, and how to 
structure agreements with collaborators or sponsors. 
 
Common Questions from 
UMKC Researchers 
What if I’m working with a company or 
another university? - Joint research can lead to 
joint IP and that can complicate things. We 
recommend establishing clear collaboration 
agreements upfront that outline who owns 
what, how decisions will be made, and who 
pays for protection costs. We can help structure 
these agreements. 
 
Can I still publish if I want a patent? - Yes - as 
long as you disclose your invention to us first 
and we file a provisional application before your work goes public. This is a common process, and 
we’ll work with your publishing timeline. 
 
What if my invention isn’t a good fit for a patent? - That’s okay. Not every innovation needs a 
patent to have value. We can help explore other forms of protection and consider licensing, trade 
secrets, or other commercialization pathways.

Taking the Next Step: What Should You Do? 
If you think you’ve created something new - or are just starting a project with commercial potential - 
reach out to us. If your work may have relevance beyond the university, here are practical next 
steps: 
1. Submit an Invention Disclosure to the Office of Technology Transfer. This confidential 
form tells us what you’ve created, when, and how. 
2. Schedule a Conversation with the Office of Commercialization to discuss possible next 
steps, including whether a market assessment, partner outreach, or startup discussion is 
warranted. 
3. Plan Your Timeline around your IP goals. If you have a paper under review or a conference 
coming up, we’ll help make sure your IP is protected in time. 
 
Conclusion: Your Innovation Deserves Protection 
Your research already has value. The question is how that value reaches beyond the university.  
 
Whether you're developing a new therapy, coding a novel algorithm, or creating a training platform, 
the right IP strategy can make all the difference in who benefits from your work and how far it goes. 
 
Let us help you navigate the process. Start with a conversation and let your innovation thrive.
