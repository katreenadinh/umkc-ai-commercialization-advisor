Founding a Research-Focused Business 
Helping you move your work beyond the university  
 
Research at UMKC often begins with discovery, but 
many projects have the potential to create impact 
beyond the university. In some cases, that impact is 
achieved by forming a new venture. 
 
This pathway is not required, and it is not the right fit 
for every innovation. However, when existing 
organizations are not well positioned to adopt or 
deploy a new idea, creating a business or nonprofit 
can become a practical way to move that work into 
real-world use. 
 
This paper outlines how to think about that decision 
and what it takes to move from research to a formed 
entity. 
 
The Evolution of Technology 
Transfer 
Technology transfer in the United States has evolved significantly over the past few decades, laying 
the groundwork for universities to commercialize their research. A major turning point was the 1980 
Bayh-Dole Act, which allowed universities to own patents from federally funded projects. This 
empowered academic institutions to create technology transfer offices to help researchers patent 
inventions and secure commercial pathways. 
 
At UMKC, the Office of Technology Transfer (OTT) is responsible for evaluating, protecting, and 
managing intellectual property. 
 
The Office of Commercialization (OC) works with faculty and graduate students to explore how 
research can move into real-world use. This includes helping assess pathways, supporting startup 
formation, and connecting researchers with external partners. 
 
These offices work together, but serve distinct roles in the translation process. By engaging with 
UMKC’s OTT and OC, researchers can lay a solid foundation for long-term business success.

Deciding to Spin Out: Key Questions 
Not every innovation should become a company. In many cases, licensing to an existing 
organization or forming a partnership is a faster and lower-risk path to impact. 
 
Forming a new venture becomes relevant when: 
• No clear industry partner exists  
• The technology requires significant development before adoption  
• The opportunity is large enough to justify building new infrastructure around it  
 
Before moving in this direction, it is important to evaluate both the opportunity and the level of 
commitment required. Consider the following: 
• Is there a clear market need? - Even the most novel technology must fulfill a real customer 
or societal requirement. Programs like NSF I-Corps or engagement with UMKC’s OC can 
help you identify genuine market opportunities.  
• Will development progress faster in academia or industry? - Early-stage technologies 
may benefit from university labs and grants. However, transitioning to a startup can 
accelerate product development and commercialization efforts.  
• Are you prepared for the demands of entrepreneurship? - Balancing academic 
responsibilities with a fast-paced startup may be challenging. High-growth companies often 
need a founder who can dedicate full-time attention to development and fundraising.  
 
By weighing these questions upfront, you gain clarity on whether spinning out a venture aligns with 
your research timeline, risk tolerance, and ultimate professional ambitions. It’s the first critical step 
in ensuring your journey is driven by realistic expectations and purposeful planning. 
 
Publication versus Protection 
Protecting intellectual property is not about limiting publication. It is about preserving the ability for 
your work to be developed, deployed, and used. 
 
When researchers make a new discovery, the drive to publish and share results is both natural and 
central to academic progress. However, public disclosures—such as conference presentations, 
poster sessions, or journal publications—can inadvertently jeopardize patent rights if made before 
securing the necessary intellectual property (IP) protections.  
 
Balancing the need for academic dissemination with the strategic timing of patent filings is crucial 
for preserving potential commercial value. To achieve this balance, consider: 
• Consult the Office of Technology Transfer (OTT) Early - If you plan to present or publish 
findings that may be patentable, contact OTT well in advance. This ensures your team has 
enough time to file a provisional or full patent application before any public disclosure. 
• Understand Grace Periods vs. Global Rules - While the United States allows a one-year 
grace period after certain disclosures, many international jurisdictions do not. If you are

• Leverage Non-Disclosure Agreements (NDAs) - In some cases, you can share details with

protects novelty if your patent has not yet been filed. 
• Plan for Conference and Journal Deadlines - Conference abstracts and journal 
submissions can be considered public disclosures. Coordinate with OTT on filing 
strategies—such as provisional patent filings—that accommodate academic timelines 
while preserving patent rights. 
• Monitor Internal Presentations - Even internal university seminars may be open to non-
UMKC audiences, effectively making them public disclosures. Confirm audience details or 
establish confidentiality requirements in advance. 
 
Being mindful of publication timelines and disclosure policies helps safeguard the novelty of your 
invention. With early coordination between you, your co-authors, and OTT, it is possible to share 
significant findings for academic advancement while still preserving the opportunity to pursue 
robust IP protections. 
 
Navigating the Technology Transfer Process 
Having established the viability of your idea and recognized the broader context of tech transfer, the 
next step involves officially safeguarding and licensing your intellectual property.  
 
At UMKC, this process begins with disclosing your invention to the Office of Technology Transfer 
(OTT) and considering how it may eventually be licensed for commercial or nonprofit use: 
• Intellectual Property Rights - If your invention was developed using UMKC resources, the 
university typically retains IP ownership. You will work with the OTT to file patents or other 
appropriate protections. 
• Licensing Agreements - To launch a startup (or nonprofit) around UMKC-owned IP, you 
must license it from the university. Negotiation terms often include royalties, equity, or both, 
and a clear agreement can be pivotal for attracting investors or grantors. 
• Legal Considerations and Timeline - IP filings and licensing negotiations take time. 
Consult legal counsel, maintain frequent communication with the OTT and OC, and plan for 
a period of back-and-forth before your venture officially forms.  
 
By engaging with technology transfer professionals early, you protect your discoveries and lay legal 
groundwork that appeals to funders and partners. This proactive approach helps prevent delays 
and sets the stage for a successful spinout or licensing arrangement. 
 
Once intellectual property is protected and a pathway has been selected, the focus shifts from 
protection to execution - building the structure, team, and strategy required to move the work 
forward.

Assembling the Founding Team 
One of the most common misconceptions is that the researcher must also serve as the CEO. In 
many cases, the most effective role for a faculty inventor is as a technical founder or advisor, not an 
operational leader. 
 
No venture thrives on technology alone. Successful startups and nonprofits require a blend of 
technical brilliance, business strategy, and day-to-day operational leadership. Building a cohesive 
founding team is often the difference between an innovation that stalls in the lab and one that 
scales in the market. 
 
A successful venture—whether a for-profit or nonprofit—requires the right blend of skills and 
experience: 
• Technical Expertise - Faculty members and graduate students intimately understand the 
science or technology behind the innovation. 
• Business Leadership - Bringing on an experienced business strategist or entrepreneur can 
ensure savvy financial planning, market outreach, and operational setup. 
• Mentors and Advisors - The UMKC Innovation Center, Missouri SBDC at UMKC, and 
Technology Venture Studio maintain extensive networks of professionals who can help 
guide first-time founders. 
 
By selecting complementary team members and mentors from the outset, you create a collective 
knowledge base that can address both technological roadblocks and strategic hurdles. In turn, this 
balanced approach boosts your venture’s credibility and momentum, especially with investors and 
early adopters. 
 
Business Structures and Formation 
Once you’ve gathered the right people, you’ll need to choose a legal entity and formalize your 
operations. The goal at this stage is not to master corporate law, but to understand the implications 
of different structures and make an informed initial choice with appropriate legal guidance. The 
business structure you select influences governance, taxation, and even the type of financing you 
can pursue, making it a pivotal step in your commercialization journey. 
 
Choosing the appropriate legal entity for your venture is foundational. Each structure carries unique 
implications for tax treatment, governance, and fundraising: 
• Common Business Structures: 
o LLC (Limited Liability Company) - Offers flexible management arrangements and 
pass-through taxation, making it a popular choice for small teams. 
o C-Corporation - Standard for high-growth startups seeking venture capital, offering 
the ability to issue multiple classes of stock. 
o S-Corporation - Provides certain tax advantages but is subject to limitations on 
ownership and shareholder types. 
o Nonprofit Organization (501(c)(3) or Similar) - Ideal for ventures focused on public 
good rather than profit distribution. Qualifying for nonprofit status can open doors to

• State of Incorporation - Many businesses incorporate in Delaware for its favorable

• Corporate Governance - Regardless of structure, establishing clear governance (board of

Choosing the right entity requires balancing your growth ambitions, funding needs, and compliance 
obligations. It’s worth seeking legal or accounting advice so you can begin on solid ground, ready for 
either investment or grant-seeking. 
 
Planning and Strategy Development 
A solid plan clarifies your mission, milestones, and resource needs. A well-structured plan or pitch 
deck is vital for communicating your venture’s direction and viability to key stakeholders—co-
founders, investors, partners, and even your internal team. Laying out coherent goals, timelines, 
and budget forecasts ensures everyone aligns on how best to move forward.  
 
Whether you create a comprehensive business plan or a concise pitch deck, ensure you address: 
• Executive Summary – One-page summary of the technology, target market or 
beneficiaries, and value proposition. 
• Market Analysis - Define your total addressable market (TAM) or community need, 
identifying potential competition and detailing your unique advantage. 
• Technology and Product (or Program) Roadmap - Map out how you will transition from 
research prototype to a fully deployable solution. Note any regulatory or compliance 
hurdles. 
• Financial Projections - Include revenue models (for for-profits) or funding strategies (for 
nonprofits), along with budget forecasts and timelines. The Missouri SBDC can help refine 
these projections. 
• Risk Assessment and Mitigation - Identify the largest technical, regulatory, or market 
uncertainties and detail strategies to minimize their impact. 
 
By investing time in a thorough plan, you create a shared vision of success that resonates with 
funders and clarifies tasks within your team. Revisit this document as you gather market feedback, 
ensuring it evolves alongside new insights and growth opportunities. 
 
Securing Funding 
Funding is not the goal. It is a tool used to reach the next meaningful milestone.

Securing capital is critical to early-stage ventures. Securing capital fuels the transition from lab 
bench to market launch. Whether you opt for grants, equity investors, or philanthropic donations, 
diversifying your funding strategy typically yields the best outcomes. 
 
A multi-pronged funding approach often proves most effective: 
• Angel Investors and Venture Capital - Many startups progress from angel funding to larger 
VC rounds as they demonstrate market traction and scalability. 
• Federal Grants (SBIR/STTR) - For tech-focused businesses, these non-dilutive funding 
sources can help move a lab prototype toward commercialization.  
• Foundation Grants and Donations (Nonprofits) - Nonprofits can tap into philanthropic 
foundations, charitable donations, and government contracts aligned with their social 
mission. 
• Local and Regional Resources - Kansas City boasts a supportive ecosystem, with 
incubators and pitch competitions regularly providing seed grants and business guidance. 
OC can help you discover which local programs fit your venture stage. 
 
Selecting the right mix of funding channels can accelerate development, fuel pilot tests, and help 
you navigate the daunting “valley of death” between early R&D and consistent revenue. Matching 
your objectives with the right investor or grant source is critical to long-term sustainability. 
 
Regulatory and Sector-Specific Guidance 
Depending on your field, navigating regulatory frameworks can either be a minor concern or a 
central focus. Certain industries—like biotechnology or aerospace—carry strict standards that 
profoundly shape product development timelines.  
 
Building a plan for compliance early prevents costly delays down the line: 
• Medical Devices and Biotech - FDA regulations (e.g., 510(k), PMA) could require lengthy 
testing phases and specialized approvals. 
• Healthcare Software - HIPAA or cybersecurity standards often apply if your technology 
handles protected health information. 
• Energy, Aerospace, and Defense - Additional certifications or security clearances may be 
needed to operate within these sectors. 
 
By factoring sector-specific regulations into your early planning, you avoid costly delays and 
enhance investor confidence. Engaging regulatory advisors or leveraging university support can 
smooth the path to compliance, ensuring your innovation meets all necessary standards.

Common Misalignments 
Early-stage ventures often struggle not because of weak technology, but because of misalignment 
between the opportunity and the approach. Common issues include: 
• Pursuing a startup when licensing would be faster  
• Building a company before validating real demand  
• Assuming the inventor must lead the business  
• Underestimating development timelines or regulatory complexity  
 
Recognizing these early can prevent wasted time and increase the likelihood of meaningful impact. 
 
Timeline and Key Milestones 
Although every venture evolves at its own pace, establishing a general roadmap helps you manage 
progress, track goals, and pivot when necessary. This timeline is illustrative, not prescriptive. 
Progress depends on the nature of the technology, market readiness, and available resources. 
 
Below is a high-level roadmap to keep your planning realistic and on track: 
• Months 0–6: Discovery & Ideation 
o File an invention disclosure with OTT 
o Begin patent application if applicable 
o Conduct initial market or community need validation 
• Months 6–12: Formation & Licensing 
o Select your legal structure (LLC, C-Corp, nonprofit, etc.) 
o Negotiate a license agreement with UMKC (if required) 
o Build the founding team 
• Months 12–18: Prototyping & Funding 
o Develop a proof-of-concept or pilot program 
o Apply for SBIR/STTR grants or solicit angel investors (for for-profits) 
o Seek philanthropic or governmental grants (for nonprofits) 
• Months 18–24: Product/Program Refinement & Growth 
o Expand beta testing or pilot projects 
o Pursue additional funding or strategic partnerships 
o Engage with the Technology Venture Studio, KCSourceLink events, or the Missouri 
SBDC to refine your go-to-market plan 
• Months 24+ 
o Launch product, service, or charitable programs to target audience 
o Scale team and operations

Conclusion 
Forming a business is one way to move research into real-world use. When aligned with the 
opportunity, it can create a focused path for development, funding, and deployment. 
 
However, it is not the default path, and it is not required for impact. 
 
By understanding how to navigate technology transfer, structure a business, secure funding, 
comply with regulations, and manage potential conflicts of interest, you set your enterprise on a 
path to sustainability. UMKC’s ecosystem, including OTT, OC, the UMKC Innovation Center, 
Missouri SBDC, KCSourceLink, and dedicated grant programs. stands ready to assist at each 
pivotal stage. With careful planning, a committed team, and these institutional resources, your 
research can make a lasting impression well beyond the university walls.
