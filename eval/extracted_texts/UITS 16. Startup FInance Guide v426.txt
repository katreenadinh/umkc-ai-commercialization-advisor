Financial Basics for UMKC Startups 
Helping you move your work beyond the university  
 
Financial planning can feel intimidating, especially if 
your training has focused on research rather than 
business. But if your work may have potential 
beyond the university, understanding a few core 
financial ideas can make a major difference. 
 
Whether your path involves a startup, licensing, 
external partnership, pilot deployment, or another 
route to use, financial planning helps you 
understand what progress will require. It helps you 
estimate costs, anticipate risks, connect resources 
to milestones, and make more informed decisions 
about what path is realistic. 
 
This whitepaper introduces the core financial 
concepts most relevant to early translation: the 
three basic financial statements, several key 
operating metrics, common funding options, and 
the importance of matching financial planning to the 
stage and pathway of your work. 
 
Where This Fits in the Translation Process 
Funding becomes relevant at multiple points in the translation process, but its role changes 
depending on the stage of the work. 
 
In early stages, funding often supports exploration and validation. This may include feasibility 
studies, proof-of-concept work, or early customer discovery. At this stage, the goal is not scale. The 
goal is reducing key uncertainties. 
 
As work progresses, funding begins to support more defined pathways. A licensing strategy, startup 
pathway, or partnership model may each require different types of capital, timelines, and levels of 
investment. 
 
Understanding funding in this way shifts the question from “How do I raise money?” to “What does 
this next step require, and what type of funding actually fits that need?”

Why Financial Planning Matters 
In academic work, we are trained to support ideas with evidence. In translation, financial planning 
is part of that evidence. It shows that you have started thinking not only about the strength of the 
idea, but also about what it would take to move that idea into use. 
 
That matters whether you are speaking with a potential licensee, an industry collaborator, an 
internal university partner, a grant reviewer, or an investor. A realistic financial model helps clarify 
the scale of the opportunity, the likely cost of progress, the timing of key decisions, and the 
resources required to reach the next milestone. 
 
Knowing how to use financial information to communicate your innovation can make a meaningful 
difference when working with partners, funders, or collaborators. It helps you connect funding to a 
specific next step, identify risks before they become constraints, and demonstrate that your project 
is being approached with discipline rather than optimism alone. 
 
Done well, financial planning does three things: 
• Translates science to business. Investors can’t compare “novel polymer” to “ML 
platform,” but they can compare gross margins and burn. 
• Flags runway gaps early. A basic cash-flow forecast tells you when to call the Missouri 
SBDC before panic sets in. 
• Builds credibility. A tidy income statement suggests you’ll keep grant funds tidy, too. 
 
Reality check: The fastest way to lose a deal isn’t a flawed patent—it’s sloppy numbers. 
 
Meet Your Core Statements 
As your work begins moving toward external use, three financial statements become increasingly 
important: the Income Statement, Balance Sheet, and Cash-Flow Statement. 
 
Think of the Income Statement, Balance Sheet, and Cash-Flow Statement as a trio of UMKC 
basketball stats. One tells you the score, one the roster, and one how gassed the players are.

Financial Implications of Different Go-to-Market Paths 
One of the most important translation decisions is identifying who will adopt, buy, implement, or 
deploy what you are creating. In some cases, that means selling to organizations. In others, it may 
involve direct consumer adoption. In still others, the path may involve licensing or institutional 
implementation rather than immediate sales. 
 
The financial structure of these paths can look very different. Customer acquisition, onboarding, 
support, and payment timing all affect what kind of model is realistic. Thinking through those 
differences early can prevent teams from choosing a path that sounds attractive but is financially 
difficult to sustain. 
 
Whether you’re pitching to hospital systems or TikTok users, success boils down to four cost 
buckets: acquisition, onboarding, collections, and support. Each behaves differently in B2B and 
B2C terrain. Model them honestly and you’ll know which go-to-market path your innovation can 
actually afford.

Funding the Next Meaningful Step 
Funding is often discussed as if it were the goal. In practice, funding is useful only if it helps a 
project reach a meaningful next milestone. That milestone might be technical validation, regulatory 
planning, customer discovery, prototype refinement, pilot deployment, or some other step that 
reduces uncertainty and improves decision-making. 
 
Different kinds of funding support different kinds of progress. Some sources preserve ownership 
but come with constraints. Others offer speed or flexibility but change control and long-term 
economics. The right funding approach depends on what you are trying to accomplish next, how 
much risk has already been reduced, and which pathway to impact appears most realistic. 
 
A useful question to ask is: What is the next concrete milestone, and what kind of capital best fits 
that milestone? 
 
Debt vs Equity in Plain English 
• Debt: Cheap control, tax-deductible interest, but you need collateral and cash flow. 
• Equity: Easier to get for pre-revenue tech, but every share you sell is a share you no longer 
own. 
 
Non-dilutive & Creative Capital 
Mix and match: 
• SBIR/STTR grants for tech milestones. 
• Pitch contests (UMKC’s Roo Idea events) for quick cash and visibility. 
• Convertible notes / SAFEs to bridge between grants and priced rounds. 
 
Map each chunk of money to a milestone before you cash the check.

Building a Model You Can Defend 
Models aren’t crystal balls; they’re 
hypothesis-testing labs. The real value is seeing 
what happens when you nudge a dial—raise 
price, delay a grant, or spike server costs.  
 
Build an integrated 3-statement model that 
updates automatically so you’re never caught 
off-guard. 
1. Start with hard data - market sizes, 
conversion rates, supplier quotes. Save 
each source in a “data room.” 
2. Mix top-down and bottom-up 
forecasting so ambition meets reality. 
3. Add supporting schedules for working 
capital: receivables, inventory, 
payables. 
4. Run at least three scenarios: base, downside, aggressive and know what triggers each. 
 
If you can’t explain an input in 30 seconds, it doesn’t belong in the model. 
 
Avoiding the Faceplants 
Every month we watch promising projects trip on the same banana peels—over-optimistic sales 
curves, forgotten support costs, or a cash crunch hiding behind healthy revenue.  
 
This quick-hit checklist will save you painful investor meetings: 
 
Pitfall Quick Fix 
Ignoring indirect costs Add cloud hosting, compliance, and support to COGS. 
Over-optimistic revenue Cross-check against market size and sales capacity. 
Under-staffing forecasts Use revenue-per-employee benchmarks as a sanity check. 
Skipping working-capital timing Build a 12-month operational cash-flow grid. 
Sparse communication Send a one-page finance dashboard to stakeholders monthly.

Conclusion 
Strong research does not automatically become real-world impact. Moving work beyond the 
university requires choices about pathway, timing, resources, and risk. Financial planning helps 
make those choices clearer. 
 
You do not need a perfect model at the outset. But you do need enough clarity to understand what 
the next step would require, what assumptions you are making, and whether the path you are 
considering is realistic. 
 
Used well, financial planning is not just a business exercise. It is part of translation. It helps you 
move from a promising idea to a more grounded understanding of what it would take for that work 
to create impact beyond the university.
